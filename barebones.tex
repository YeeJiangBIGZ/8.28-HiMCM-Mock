{\Large\bfseries Planning Public EV Charging:\\[4pt] Waiting, Revenue, and Phased Investment}
\end{center}

In five years, the number of registered electric vehicles in Springfield increased
from 320 to 2,180. The city's 41 existing charging ports are distributed unevenly:
Downtown has twelve, whereas Riverside has none. GreenCharge must choose which
stations to build under a limited initial budget and prepare for further growth
through 2030. An effective plan must distinguish the number of EVs from the number
seeking public charging at the same time.

We develop a demand model in which registered EVs generate public-charging visits
within their own districts. Weekly visit frequency determines demand volume;
measured session records inform its timing and the time for which a vehicle occupies
a port. A peak/off-peak approximation represents recurring congestion without
requiring a separate detailed traffic model. External records provide evidence for
charging patterns, while local demand scale remains a distinct calibration input.

A time-varying queue simulation evaluates each construction option. Vehicles enter
a first-come, first-served queue and begin charging whenever a port becomes available.
Peak demand may exceed service capacity: queues grow during overload and can decline
when demand subsides. We measure the area under the queue as total waiting time and
report the unfinished queue at the observation boundary. This gives districts with
insufficient capacity an explicit service deficit, including those with no chargers.

Construction utility combines two benefits: waiting hours saved and additional
charging receipts. TOPSIS measures proximity to the best attainable combination of
these outcomes. Installation budgets remain explicit constraints, and maintenance
and operating costs enter a separate financial assessment. This distinguishes a
socially useful investment from a financially self-supporting one.

We retain an ARIMA$(1,2,0)$ registration forecast: 3,242 EVs in 2025, 6,083 in 2027,
and 11,915 in 2030. Each of ten candidate sites can open in one of three phases or
remain unbuilt, giving at most $4^{10}=1,048,576$ schedules. District-level simulation
results can be reused across these schedules because charging demand is local.
The nine-district, five-mile graph is connected; Downtown and University have the
highest degree. A new station in every district requires at least \$3.13 million
in installation spending across all phases.

Numerical rankings require local public-visit frequency, suitable service-duration
distributions, charging receipts, and criterion weights. Registrations alone cannot
identify an optimal schedule.

\section{Introduction}
\subsection{Background and Problem Restatement}
A charging visit can occupy a port for hours. A district may therefore have enough
available charging time over a week and still experience substantial queues during
a concentrated peak. Both arrival timing and occupation duration matter for planning.

Springfield contains nine districts, 120,000 residents, and 2,180 registered EVs in
2024. GreenCharge has ten candidate sites with specified construction costs,
maintenance costs, and charging capacities. The initial investment is limited to
\$2 million and four to six stations. Expansion is divided into 2025--2026,
2027--2028, and 2029--2030. The city's requests lead to three linked questions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Where would additional public charging produce the greatest benefit, and how
will EV registrations change over the planning horizon?
\item Which affordable combination of candidate stations should be built, considering
service, revenue, and the supplied geographical network?
\item When should the remaining investments occur as demand grows, and what service
and financial consequences follow from the resulting schedule?
\end{enumerate}
These questions share a single demand model. Future construction changes the queues
in later years, so the initial decision and expansion decisions must be evaluated
together.

\subsection{Model Overview}
Our analysis follows four steps. First, we translate district registrations into
public visits and estimate recurring arrival patterns. Second, we simulate how these
visits use existing and proposed ports. Third, we compare each option with a common
no-construction baseline, obtaining waiting savings and additional revenue. Finally,
we score these benefits with TOPSIS and enumerate feasible construction schedules.

The forecast supplies growth, the queue model evaluates service, and TOPSIS expresses
construction utility. Enumeration enforces the investment constraints. The district
graph also measures the effect of requiring an early investment in a central location.

\section{System Boundary}
We interpret the listed chargers as public charging ports, each serving one EV at a
time. A station is a site containing several such ports. The study covers the nine
districts and the ten supplied candidate sites. Public charging frequency is defined
per registered EV; it includes EVs that rarely use public facilities without requiring
an inventory of household chargers.

We assume that an EV's public-charging requests occur in its registration district.
The available data do not identify commuting destinations or willingness to detour
for charging. This local-demand approximation makes each district's service history
separable, but it can underrepresent visitor demand in employment and shopping areas.
All alternatives are evaluated under this same boundary.

\subsection{From Registered EVs to Hourly Arrivals}
\label{sec:arrivals}
Let $\nu_i$ denote the average number of public visits generated by one registered EV
in one week. The expected weekly volume in district $i$ is
\begin{equation}
V_{i,y}=E_{i,y}\nu_i.\label{eq:visits}
\end{equation}
For example, the same registered fleet produces twice as many visits if its public
charging frequency doubles. The definition averages over all registered EVs, including
those that seldom visit public chargers. It does not require every battery to be nearly
empty at arrival or fully charged at departure.

\textbf{Arrival approximation.} We represent the week by recurring peak and off-peak
blocks. Let $P_i\subset[0,168)$ be the peak-hour set, $H_i$ its duration in hours, and
$p_i$ the share of weekly visits occurring in that set. Assuming continuous access,
\begin{equation}
\lambda_{i,y}(h)=
\begin{cases}
E_{i,y}\nu_i p_i/H_i,&h\in P_i,\\[2pt]
E_{i,y}\nu_i(1-p_i)/(168-H_i),&h\notin P_i.
\end{cases}\label{eq:arrival}
\end{equation}
Both expressions have units of visits per hour. The numerators divide weekly demand
between the two blocks, and the denominators distribute each part over its available
time. Thus
\begin{equation}
\int_0^{168}\lambda_{i,y}(h)\,dh
=E_{i,y}\nu_i[p_i+(1-p_i)]=V_{i,y}.\label{eq:conserve}
\end{equation}
This conservation check prevents a narrower peak from accidentally creating or deleting
weekly demand. A four-hour peak occurring on five weekdays contributes twenty hours
to $H_i$; the clock location of each interval is retained in $P_i$.

We assume arrivals form a nonhomogeneous Poisson process. In an interval $[a,b)$,
its arrival count has mean $\int_a^b\lambda_i(u)\,du$, and counts in disjoint intervals
are independent. Within a constant-rate block of length $\Delta$, we draw a Poisson
count with mean $\lambda\Delta$ and place those arrivals uniformly in the block.
This permits random clustering around the expected pattern.

The Poisson assumption describes charging requests under a specified demand scenario.
A vehicle can generate more than one visit during the observation horizon, so queue
counts represent requests rather than a census of distinct vehicles. Independence is
an approximation; systematic changes in driver behavior after prolonged congestion
would require a different demand model.

Peak duration alone is insufficient. A short peak containing only a small share of
visits may be easier to serve than a longer peak containing most of the week's demand.
Equation~\ref{eq:arrival} therefore retains both duration and demand share. Where access
is restricted, the same construction applies over opening hours, with zero arrivals
outside the assumed access window.

For $n$ source sessions, let $n_h$ be the number beginning in weekly hour $h$.
The empirical arrival share is $\widehat q_h=n_h/n$, so
$\sum_{h=0}^{167}\widehat q_h=1$. Given a proposed set of whole-hour peak bins,
\begin{equation}
\widehat p_i=\sum_{h\in P_i}\widehat q_h,\qquad H_i=|P_i|.
\label{eq:fitpeak}
\end{equation}
Here $H_i$ is measured in hours because each bin is one hour. A reproducible way to
choose the blocks is to compare contiguous daily windows and minimize the squared
difference between $\widehat q_h$ and their two-level approximation. Weekday and weekend
windows can be fitted separately when their patterns differ. This uses source data
to determine concentration while keeping the final model compact.

For session $k$, occupation duration is $D_k=b_k-a_k$, where $a_k$ is connection time
and $b_k$ is port-release time. The empirical distribution
\begin{equation}
\widehat F_i(d)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^n\ind\{D_k\le d\}
\label{eq:duration}
\end{equation}
allows service durations to vary. Sampling within the corresponding arrival block
can preserve differences between daytime and overnight visits. A transaction end time
is a proxy for release when unplug time is unavailable, as in the Jiaxing records.

These samples describe their original venues. Their different mean intervals should
not be pooled without checking charger speed, access, and parking behavior. Residential,
workplace, and retail profiles are candidate proxies for similar Springfield venues;
mixed districts require a stated mixture or a tested single-profile approximation.
Normalized source timing supplies the shape of demand. It does not identify
$\nu_i$, which requires public visits and registered-EV exposure for a comparable
catchment. Consequently, the source sample sizes are not used as Springfield demand
weights or as estimates of local adoption.

\subsection{Forecasting EV Registrations}
\label{sec:forecast}
Springfield's annual registrations are 320, 410, 580, 860, 1,380, and 2,180 for
2019--2024. We use an ARIMA time-series model as the primary projection and a logistic
curve as a growth comparison. The forecast changes the scale of charging arrivals;
its district allocation is specified below.

Let $C_y$ be citywide registrations and define the second difference
$X_y=C_y-2C_{y-1}+C_{y-2}$. This is the change in the annual increase, rather than
the registration level itself. The selected ARIMA$(1,2,0)$ model is
\begin{equation}
X_y=\phi_1X_{y-1}+\epsilon_y,\qquad
C_y=2C_{y-1}-C_{y-2}+X_y.\label{eq:arima}
\end{equation}
The first expression forecasts the change in growth using its previous value;
the second reconstructs registrations. The fit selected from $p,q\in\{0,1\}$ by
minimum AIC has AIC 52.07, $\widehat\phi_1=0.935$, and residual standard deviation
79 vehicles. With only four second differences, diagnostic tests have little power.
A 25,000-vehicle planning ceiling lies above the forecasts through 2030 and therefore
does not change the results in Table~\ref{tab:forecast}.

The comparison model is
\begin{equation}
C_y=\frac{K}{1+\exp[-r(y-y_0)]}.\label{eq:logistic}
\end{equation}
Here $K$ is the saturation level, $r$ controls growth speed, and $y_0$ is the year
at which the curve reaches $K/2$. This form imposes eventual saturation. The short
observed series does not establish a saturation level, so the logistic fit is used
as an alternative growth scenario rather than evidence of a known carrying capacity.

\caption{Citywide registration forecasts.}\label{tab:forecast}

\textbf{District allocation.} In the absence of district adoption histories, we hold
the 2024 registration shares fixed:
\begin{equation}
E_{i,y}=\frac{E_{i,2024}}{2180}C_y.\label{eq:districtgrowth}
\end{equation}
The district forecasts sum to $C_y$. Fractional values are expected counts and need
not be rounded before computing arrival rates. Holding $\nu_i$ and timing fixed,
public visits scale by the same factor as registrations: approximately 1.49 in 2025
and 5.47 in 2030 relative to 2024. These factors show why capacity adequate initially
may become congested later. They do not, by themselves, establish the year when any
particular station should open.

\subsection{A Queue Model for Temporary Overload}
\label{sec:queue}
\textbf{Local pooling.} Within each district, we approximate available public ports
by a single first-come, first-served queue. This assumes drivers can use the district's
available facilities effectively. It avoids estimating individual site catchments
without within-district travel data, but may understate delay where ports are far apart
or access is restricted.

Let $a_k$ be the arrival time of request $k$, $s_k$ its service-start time, and $D_k$
its sampled occupation duration. Its completion time is
\begin{equation}
b_k=s_k+D_k,\qquad w_k=s_k-a_k.\label{eq:service}
\end{equation}
Thus waiting ends when charging can begin, while the port remains occupied until
$b_k$. Occupation may include time after electricity delivery if the vehicle has not
yet released the port. We assume durations are not shortened simply because a queue
has formed.

For a fixed capacity, keep each port's next available time $v_j$. Process requests
in arrival order and select the earliest available port:
\begin{equation}
j^*=\arg\min_j v_j,\qquad
s_k=\max\{a_k,v_{j^*}\},\qquad v_{j^*}\leftarrow s_k+D_k.
\label{eq:recursion}
\end{equation}
If a port is free, the maximum returns the arrival time and waiting is zero. If every
port is occupied, the request waits until the earliest release. A new port enters the
availability list at its opening time. With no available ports, requests remain queued
until a later opening; if none occurs, they remain unfinished at the horizon.

An event simulation implements these rules using arrivals, completions, and site
openings. At a common timestamp, completions and openings are processed before waiting
requests are assigned to free ports. Unfinished service and waiting queues carry across
peak boundaries, midnight, year boundaries, and construction phases. We assume no
exogenous initial queue at the common start of observation; all alternatives use the
same initial state.

For interpretation, let $d_i=\E[D_i]$. With $c$ comparable ports, the approximate
saturated service rate is $\mu_i(c)=c/d_i$ visits per hour. When peak arrivals exceed
this rate, a queue can grow. When arrivals later fall below it, spare capacity can
clear the backlog. It is unnecessary to require service capacity to exceed every
peak, which may be unaffordable.

The simulation uses finite observation periods and does not require a stationary
queue. In particular, an $M/M/c$ equilibrium waiting formula cannot describe a period
of sustained overload. Our model also permits empirical occupation durations instead
of requiring an exponential distribution. No arrival is removed after a fixed waiting
time: uncompleted demand remains visible in the service accounting.

\subsection{Waiting, Completed Service, and Backlog}
\label{sec:metrics}
Let $Q_i(u)$ be the number of requests waiting, excluding vehicles already occupying
ports. Over a common horizon $[0,T]$, total waiting is
\begin{equation}
W_i(T)=\E\!\left[\int_0^T Q_i(u)\,du\right]
=\E\!\left[\sum_{a_k<T}\bigl(\min\{s_k,T\}-a_k\bigr)\right].
\label{eq:waiting}
\end{equation}
Set $s_k=+\infty$ for a request that has not started service. The integral counts
one vehicle-hour whenever one request waits for one hour. Equivalently, each request
contributes its observed waiting interval. A request still queued at $T$ contributes
$T-a_k$; it is not omitted because its eventual wait is unknown \cite{mitqueue}.

We also report expected completed visits $S_i(T)$, terminal waiting backlog $B_i(T)$,
and occupied ports $I_i(T)$. For an initially empty system, every realization satisfies
\begin{equation}
A_i(T)=S_i(T)+Q_i(T)+I_i(T),\qquad B_i(T)=\E[Q_i(T)],
\label{eq:balance}
\end{equation}
where $A_i(T)$ is the number of arrivals by $T$. This is a conservation check: every
request has completed, is waiting, or is in service. Revenue is associated with completed
visits under the billing convention in Section~\ref{sec:finance}.

The reported ratio $W_i(T)/\E[A_i(T)]$ is accrued waiting per arriving request. It is
not the eventual mean wait if a backlog remains. For a district with no ports throughout
the horizon, $S_i(T)=0$ and
\begin{equation}
B_i(T)=\int_0^T\lambda_i(u)\,du,\qquad
W_i(T)=\int_0^T(T-u)\lambda_i(u)\,du.\label{eq:zero}
\end{equation}
Earlier arrivals contribute more waiting because they remain unserved longer. This
provides a finite, explicitly horizon-dependent comparison without inventing departures.

A simple fluid calculation explains why peak length matters. Suppose arrivals occur
at constant rate $\lambda_p>\mu$ for $H$ hours, starting from an empty queue. The
end-of-peak queue is approximately $(\lambda_p-\mu)H$. If the following arrival rate
is $\lambda_o<\mu$, the clearance time is
\begin{equation}
t_{\rm clear}=\frac{(\lambda_p-\mu)H}{\mu-\lambda_o},\qquad
W_{\rm fluid}=\frac{(\lambda_p-\mu)H}{2}(H+t_{\rm clear}).
\label{eq:fluid}
\end{equation}
The waiting estimate is the area of the queue's growth and recovery triangles. It
applies only when the off-peak block lasts long enough to clear the queue. Otherwise,
the remaining backlog carries forward. This calculation is explanatory; the event
simulation accounts for random arrivals and durations when comparing investments.

\subsection{Combining Service and Revenue Benefits}
\label{sec:topsis}
For each district and year, compare a construction history $\ell$ with the same demand
under no new construction, denoted by 0. Measure outcomes over that calendar year's
interval while preserving the queue inherited from earlier years. The two benefits are
\begin{equation}
b_{i,y,1}(\ell)=W_{i,y}(0)-W_{i,y}(\ell),\qquad
b_{i,y,2}(\ell)=R_{i,y}(\ell)-R_{i,y}(0).
\label{eq:benefits}
\end{equation}
Waiting savings have units of vehicle-hours; revenue gains have units of dollars.
The common baseline ensures that all benefits are attributable to construction under
the specified demand. Existing and new ports are evaluated together, so two Downtown
sites do not each receive credit for removing the same queue.

TOPSIS ranks alternatives by closeness to an ideal combination of both benefits
\cite{topsis}. To make scores comparable across districts and years, define a single
reference set containing all evaluated local histories and their no-build baselines.
For criterion $j$, let $L_j$ and $H_j$ be its minimum and maximum over this set. Then
\begin{equation}
z_{i,y,j}(\ell)=\frac{b_{i,y,j}(\ell)-L_j}{H_j-L_j},\qquad
v_{i,y,j}(\ell)=\omega_jz_{i,y,j}(\ell),\quad
\omega_1+\omega_2=1.\label{eq:normalization}
\end{equation}
Normalization places both outcomes on a common scale, while positive weights
$\omega_j$ express the Council's preferences. A constant criterion has no discriminatory
information and is omitted, with remaining weights rescaled. The reference scales are
held fixed when comparing schedules within one analysis.

The ideal weighted point is $v^+=(\omega_1,\omega_2)$ and the negative ideal is
$v^-=(0,0)$. Define
\begin{equation}
D^\pm=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{2}(v_{i,y,j}-v_j^\pm)^2},\qquad
U_{i,y}(\ell)=100\frac{D^-}{D^++D^-}.\label{eq:topsis}
\end{equation}
A high score means proximity to the ideal and distance from the negative ideal.
If all criteria are constant, all alternatives are tied. The distance construction
can favor a compromise, but it does not guarantee that every balanced alternative
outranks every specialized one; the measured benefits, scales, and weights determine
that comparison.

District priority can be reported by evaluating the same additional capacity $m_*$
in each district over a common reference period. Equal added capacity isolates where
capacity is most beneficial; actual site selection then uses the capacities and costs
of the supplied candidates. Neither the benchmark size nor the criterion weights can
be inferred from registration counts. Numerical scores are conditional on these stated
policy choices and on the demand and revenue parameters.

\subsection{Phased Capacity and Budget Constraints}
\label{sec:constraints}
Let $z_s\in\{0,1,2,3\}$ specify site $s$'s construction phase; zero means unbuilt.
The phases cover 2025--2026, 2027--2028, and 2029--2030. We assume a chosen site is
available at the beginning of its phase and remains available thereafter. Let $t(y)$
be the phase containing year $y$, and let $\mathcal S_i$ contain district $i$'s sites.
Available capacity is
\begin{equation}
c_i(y;z)=c_i^0+\sum_{s\in\mathcal S_i}M_s
\ind\{1\le z_s\le t(y)\}.\label{eq:capacity}
\end{equation}
The indicator equals one only after the site's opening phase. Hence a Phase-1 station
also contributes service in Phases 2 and 3, without a second installation payment.
The service simulation updates capacity at these opening events.

Let $B_t$ be the installation budget in phase $t$. Feasibility requires
\begin{equation}
\sum_{s=1}^{10}C_s\ind\{z_s=t\}\le B_t\quad(t=1,2,3),\qquad
4\le\sum_{s=1}^{10}\ind\{z_s=1\}\le6.\label{eq:budget}
\end{equation}
The first-phase budget is $B_1=\$2,000,000$. Later appropriations are policy inputs;
the statement does not specify their amounts. These constraints concern installation
funds, while annual maintenance is accounted for in the financial analysis.

The instruction to build in every district cannot hold simultaneously with a
four-to-six-station initial phase across nine districts. We interpret district-wide
construction coverage as a terminal requirement:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{s\in\mathcal S_i}\ind\{z_s>0\}\ge1,
\qquad i=1,\ldots,9.\label{eq:coverage}
\end{equation}
This requires at least nine new stations by the end of Phase 3. It establishes local
presence, not a guarantee of short queues. The cheapest such collection is S2 together
with S3--S10, costing \$3.13 million and adding 66 ports. Therefore $B_2+B_3$ must be
at least \$1.13 million if the initial phase can use its entire budget. This is a
necessary funding bound; phase-specific restrictions may require more.

If the available later budgets fail the coverage requirement, enumeration returns an
infeasible plan rather than silently relaxing it. The Council can then change funding
or the coverage target. No constraint requires capacity to exceed peak arrivals:
affordable schedules with temporary overload remain eligible and are compared by their
service and revenue benefits. The five-mile graph is assessed in Section~\ref{sec:graph}
without transferring charging requests between districts.

\section{Financial Assessment and Joint Schedule Selection}
\subsection{Revenue and Break-Even}
\label{sec:finance}
We use the same completed visits in service and financial calculations. Let $r_i$ be
the average receipt per completed public visit in district $i$. With payment recognized
on completion, annual gross receipts are
\begin{equation}
R_{i,y}(z)=r_iS_{i,y}(z),\qquad
\Delta R_{i,y}=r_i\,[S_{i,y}(z)-S_{i,y}(0)].\label{eq:revenue}
\end{equation}
Here $S_{i,y}$ counts completions during year $y$, including visits that arrived earlier.
A vehicle still waiting, or still occupying a port at the year boundary, produces no
completed-visit receipt in that year under this convention.

Additional capacity may increase receipts within the planning horizon by completing
visits sooner. Because requests do not leave the queue, it need not create additional
lifetime purchases if every request would eventually be served under both alternatives.
The revenue criterion therefore measures additional receipts within the stated horizon.
This distinction is especially important when comparing a short observation window
with a long investment life.

Let $g_i$ be average electricity and other variable cost per completed visit and
$m_s$ annual site maintenance. The incremental annual operating contribution is
\begin{equation}
\Delta\Pi_y=\sum_i(r_i-g_i)\,[S_{i,y}(z)-S_{i,y}(0)]
-\sum_{s:\,1\le z_s\le t(y)}m_s.\label{eq:profit}
\end{equation}
Unchanged maintenance costs for existing ports cancel in the incremental comparison. Construction utility uses gross receipts as its financial criterion;
Equation~\ref{eq:profit} separately shows whether the additional service supports the
operating costs of the proposed investments.

For a capital-recovery check, let $L$ be investment life and $d$ the annual discount
rate. The annualization factor is
\begin{equation}
a(d,L)=\begin{cases}
\dfrac{d(1+d)^L}{(1+d)^L-1},&d>0,\\[4pt]
1/L,&d=0.
\end{cases}\label{eq:annuity}
\end{equation}
For a group of new sites $G$ in one district and positive per-visit margin
$r_i-g_i$, the incremental completions needed annually to cover annualized capital
and maintenance are
\begin{equation}
S_{i,G}^{\rm BE}=\frac{a(d,L)\sum_{s\in G}C_s+\sum_{s\in G}m_s}{r_i-g_i}.
\label{eq:breakeven}
\end{equation}
The numerator is dollars per year and the denominator dollars per visit, producing
visits per year. Comparing this threshold with additional simulated completions tests
incremental self-financing. A socially valuable station can fail this test; the outcome
then identifies a subsidy requirement rather than proving that the station should
never be built. Prices, variable costs, and investment life must be specified before
numerical break-even claims are made.

\subsection{Exhaustive Search over Complete Schedules}
\label{sec:search}
With four choices for each of ten sites, the complete search space has
$4^{10}=1,048,576$ schedules before feasibility filtering. This size permits direct
enumeration. Joint evaluation allows an early investment to be judged by both its
immediate benefit and its effect on later congestion.

A capacity count alone is insufficient for a later-year lookup. Two histories with
the same current ports may have different queues because one opened earlier. Instead,
we precompute complete \emph{local construction histories}. Downtown has two sites,
so it has $4^2=16$ histories; each of the other eight districts has four. Thus only
\begin{equation}
4^2+8\cdot4=48\label{eq:histories}
\end{equation}
local histories require simulation for each demand scenario and random replication.
Each runs continuously from the start of 2025 to the end of 2030. Its annual outputs
retain inherited queues, ongoing service, completions, receipts, and terminal backlog.

For schedule $z$, let $\ell_i(z)$ be district $i$'s local history. The citywide objective is
\begin{equation}
F(z)=\sum_{y=2025}^{2030}\beta_y\sum_{i=1}^{9}
\left[U_{i,y}\bigl(\ell_i(z)\bigr)-U_{i,y}(0)\right].\label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
The nonnegative year weights $\beta_y$ state the time preference; equal weights treat
each year's utility equally. Subtracting the baseline makes the no-construction
reference explicit. Each district's combined benefit is counted once per year. The
objective is a sum of comparable utility scores, not a sum of dollars and waiting
hours. Different year weights or TOPSIS weights represent different policy preferences.

\begin{enumerate}
\item Generate identical arrival times and requested occupation durations for all
local histories of a district in a simulation replication.
\item Simulate the 48 local histories, compute annual benefits, and fix the common
TOPSIS normalization scales. Store utility and physical outcomes together.
\item Enumerate the complete city schedules. Reject those violating budgets, initial
station count, confirmed site availability, or terminal district coverage.
\item For each feasible schedule, retrieve its nine histories and evaluate
Equation~\ref{eq:objective}. Retain the maximum; resolve numerical ties by lower total
installation cost and then a fixed site-order rule.
\end{enumerate}

Using common random inputs reduces noise in comparisons. Repeated replications estimate
expected benefits; increasing their number should stabilize close rankings before a
construction recommendation is made. Enumeration is exact for the declared feasible
set and estimated objective, while simulation and demand assumptions remain sources
of uncertainty. The chosen schedule should be accompanied by annual waiting, completed
visits, backlog, receipts, maintenance, and cumulative ports so that its score can be
interpreted in physical terms.

\subsection{District Connectivity and Central-Site Requirement}
\label{sec:graph}
The problem also asks for a graph based on the supplied inter-district distances.
Let $d_{ij}$ be the distance in miles between districts $i$ and $j$. Its adjacency
matrix and degree are
\begin{equation}
A_{ij}=\ind\{i\ne j,\ d_{ij}\le5\},\qquad
k_i=\sum_{j=1}^{9}A_{ij}.\label{eq:graph}
\end{equation}
An edge identifies a pair within five miles, and degree counts direct neighbors.
It does not give a charging probability or establish that a driver will travel to a
neighboring district when local ports are full.
\begin{table}[h]\centering\small
\caption{Adjacency results from the five-mile rule.}\label{tab:graph}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lp{0.56\textwidth}r@{}}
\toprule District & Neighbors & Degree\\\midrule
Downtown &Southside, Eastside, University, Mall District&4\\
Northside &Westside, University&2\\
Southside &Downtown, Industrial&2\\
Eastside &Downtown, University, Mall District&3\\
Westside &Northside, Riverside&2\\
University &Downtown, Northside, Eastside, Mall District&4\\
Industrial &Southside&1\\
Mall District &Downtown, Eastside, University&3\\
Riverside &Westside&1\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}\end{table}

The degrees sum to 22, giving eleven undirected edges. Starting at Downtown and
repeatedly visiting neighboring districts reaches all nine nodes, so the graph has
one connected component. Downtown and University tie for maximum degree at four.
Industrial and Riverside each have one direct neighbor. Connectivity means that a
path exists through the graph; it does not mean every district is within five miles
of every other district.

To quantify the consequence of requiring an early central investment, repeat the
same enumeration with
\begin{equation}
\ind\{z_{\mathrm{S1}}=1\}+\ind\{z_{\mathrm{S2}}=1\}
+\ind\{z_{\mathrm{S7}}=1\}\ge1.\label{eq:central}
\end{equation}
This places at least one Phase-1 station in either maximum-degree district. If the
requirement names a particular tied district, restrict the condition to that district's
sites. Let $F^*$ be the unrestricted maximum and $F^*_{\rm central}$ the maximum under
this extra condition. Then $F^*-F^*_{\rm central}\ge0$ measures the utility cost of the
requirement whenever the restricted set is feasible.

A zero difference means that a best schedule already satisfies the centrality policy.
A positive difference shows the service-and-revenue benefit forgone by imposing it.
This assessment preserves the local charging-demand assumption while answering the
geographical question with the same construction choices and budget limits.
