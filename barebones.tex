{\large\bfseries Community EV Charging: A Construction Model}\\[4pt]
{\small Working draft: Sections 1--5}
\end{center}
\noindent\textit{Draft status.} This copy defines the revised model and search procedure.
It preserves the existing EV forecast, but does not report new district rankings or a
construction schedule. Charging frequency, profile transfer, waiting tolerance, prices
and preference weights still require calibration or an explicit scenario choice.
The original Section 6 is outside this revision.

\section{Introduction}
\subsection{Background and Problem Restatement}
Springfield's EV registrations rose from 320 in 2019 to 2,180 in 2024. Its nine
districts have 41 listed chargers, distributed unevenly: Downtown has 12 for 420 EVs,
while Riverside has none for 120. GreenCharge must decide where additional public
charging capacity would be most useful and when it should be installed.

The problem asks us to assess district demand, forecast EV registrations, select four
to six initial stations within a \$2 million budget, and plan expansion through 2030.
It also requires a district-connectivity analysis and a recommendation to the Mayor.
We evaluate construction using two outcomes: total waiting time saved and additional
charging revenue. These outcomes describe the benefits the new stations would deliver.

\subsection{Our Approach}
We first convert registered EVs into public-charging arrivals and simulate the queues
at different capacities. TOPSIS combines the resulting waiting-time and revenue
benefits into a construction utility. We then examine all feasible assignments of the
ten candidate sites to three construction phases. Initial construction and expansion
are chosen together, using the same model throughout.

\section{Model Scope}
We model public charging within the nine supplied districts and select from the ten
listed sites. Household charging infrastructure is outside the model. Behavioral and
planning assumptions are introduced beside the equations they support, with a short
justification; they are not repeated in a separate assumption catalogue.

\section{Evaluating Construction Benefits}
\subsection{District Demand and Arrival Timing}
\label{sec:arrivals}
\textbf{Assumption: local public demand.} EVs generate public-charging visits only in
the district where they are registered. This avoids estimating commuting and charging
detours without destination data. We interpret the problem's existing chargers as
public charging ports, each able to serve one EV at a time; the table does not establish
an inventory of household chargers.

The expected number of public visits in a week is
\begin{equation}
V_{i,y}=E_{i,y}\nu_i.
\label{eq:visits}
\end{equation}
The frequency $\nu_i$ is averaged across all registered EVs, including those that
rarely use public charging. Registrations therefore do not imply that every EV arrives
once per week. Population and road traffic are not additional scores: they cannot
identify public visits without further behavioral information.

\textbf{Assumption: two arrival levels.} We approximate the week by peak and off-peak
blocks to retain demand concentration without fitting a detailed curve. Let $P_i$ be
the set of peak hours, $H_i$ its total duration, and $p_i$ the fraction of weekly visits
in those hours. Assuming continuous access and $0<H_i<168$,
\begin{equation}
\lambda_{i,y}(h)=
\begin{cases}
V_{i,y}p_i/H_i, & h\in P_i,\\
V_{i,y}(1-p_i)/(168-H_i), & h\notin P_i.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:arrival-rate}
\end{equation}
Thus the integral of the arrival rate over one week is $V_{i,y}$. A repeated daily
peak contributes its duration on each applicable day; weekday and weekend windows
can differ. Restricted opening hours would require a different accessible-hour set.

Published workplace and venue-based session records provide candidate timing and
occupation-duration profiles \cite{NRELWorkplace,KoreaCharging2024,JiaxingCharging2025}.
Their normalized timing, rather than raw session counts, can be transferred. We use
district type only to select a suitable profile, not to assign a propensity score.
The source's access rules and charger hardware must match the intended use. A profile
borrowed for a mixed or emerging district is a proxy and must be identified as such.
The current draft leaves these mappings and numerical peak parameters unset.

\subsection{EV Registration Forecast}
\label{sec:forecast}
The existing forecast is retained as the demand input. The citywide series $C_y$
is modeled by ARIMA, with a logistic curve as a comparison. The original analysis
differences the series twice and selects ARIMA$(1,2,0)$ from $p,q\in\{0,1\}$ by
minimum AIC. In general,
\begin{equation}
\nabla^d C_y=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_j\nabla^d C_{y-j}
+\epsilon_y+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\theta_j\epsilon_{y-j}.
\label{eq:arima}
\end{equation}
The retained fit has AIC $52.07$, $\hat\phi_1=0.935$, and residual standard deviation
79 vehicles. Only four second-differenced observations are available, so diagnostic
evidence is limited. The original 25,000-vehicle business ceiling is retained; it does
not affect the reported forecasts through 2030.

The comparison model retains the logistic form
\begin{equation}
C_y=\frac{K}{1+e^{-r(y-y_0)}},
\label{eq:logistic}
\end{equation}
where $K$ is the saturation level, $r$ the growth parameter and $y_0$ the midpoint.
The adopted forecast remains ARIMA; no model is refitted in this draft.

\textbf{Assumption: fixed district shares.} Without district-level adoption histories,
we distribute each citywide forecast in proportion to the 2024 registrations:
\begin{equation}
E_{i,y}=E_{i,2024}\frac{C_y}{2180}.
\label{eq:district-forecast}
\end{equation}
Charging frequency and timing are held fixed across years in the base model, so growth
changes arrival volume. This is a planning assumption, not a prediction of unchanged
charging habits. The resulting arrivals enter the same queue model for every year.

\subsection{Queue Simulation}
\label{sec:queues}
\textbf{Assumption: pooled local service.} We treat the available ports in a district
as one first-come, first-served queue. This avoids modeling individual parking locations
without within-district distances. It assumes drivers can use the district's available
ports effectively and may understate delay when those ports are difficult to reach.

Arrivals follow a Poisson process with the piecewise rate in Equation~\ref{eq:arrival-rate};
this permits random arrivals while keeping the expected peak volume fixed. Each service
occupies one port for a sampled duration $D_i$. We use occupation until the port is
released, rather than electricity-delivery time alone. Transaction duration is only a
proxy where unplug time is unavailable. Matching the source hardware and public access
is part of calibration.

\textbf{Provisional assumption: finite patience.} To compare overloaded districts and
a district with no ports, an arrival waits at most $\tau$ hours before leaving unserved.
This simple rule prevents infinite queues and makes unmet demand explicit. The value
of $\tau$ is not yet chosen. Service starts immediately when a port becomes available;
vehicles do not wait for the peak to end as a group.

For a weekly arrival cohort, let $\mathcal S$ contain served visits, let $w_k$ be their
waiting times, and let $A$ count visits that leave after $\tau$. The simulation estimates
\begin{equation}
W_{i,y}(c)=\mathbb E\!\left[\sum_{k\in\mathcal S}w_k+\tau A\right],
\qquad
S_{i,y}(c)=\mathbb E[|\mathcal S|].
\label{eq:queue-outcomes}
\end{equation}
Unserved visits $\mathbb E[A]$ are also reported separately. In particular, zero ports
imply zero served visits and $W_{i,y}(0)=\tau V_{i,y}$, not zero waiting.

An event simulation processes arrivals, service completions and patience deadlines.
Queues and occupied ports carry across hourly and daily boundaries. A warm-up precedes
the recorded week, and processing continues until its arrivals are served or abandon;
no queue is erased at the reporting boundary. Repeated runs estimate the expectations.
We compare capacities using the same sampled arrivals, occupation times and patience.
Temporary overload is allowed even when $\lambda_{i,y}(h)>c/\mathbb E[D_i]$; the simulation
measures the resulting delay rather than applying a stationary waiting-time formula.

\subsection{Two-Criterion TOPSIS Utility}
\label{sec:utility}
For a fixed district and year, compare capacity $c$ with its existing capacity $c_i^0$:
\begin{equation}
\Delta W_{i,y}(c)=W_{i,y}(c_i^0)-W_{i,y}(c),\qquad
\Delta R_{i,y}(c)=R_{i,y}(c)-R_{i,y}(c_i^0).
\label{eq:benefits}
\end{equation}
Both comparisons use identical demand. A charger does not create a paying visit merely
by being installed.

\textbf{Assumption: revenue per completed visit.} With average charging receipt $r_i$
per completed visit, $R_{i,y}(c)=r_iS_{i,y}(c)$. This keeps the financial criterion on the
same session basis as the service model. It is gross charging revenue; electricity,
maintenance and capital costs belong in the later financial assessment. If all demand
is already served, extra capacity can save waiting without increasing revenue.

TOPSIS compares the two outcomes with an ideal and a worst reference
\cite{Hwang1981}. Write an option as $a=(i,y,c)$ and its two benefit values as
$b_{a1}=\Delta W_{i,y}(c)$ and $b_{a2}=\Delta R_{i,y}(c)$. Across the declared reference
set of district--year--capacity options, define
\begin{equation}
z_{aj}=\frac{b_{aj}-L_j}{H_j-L_j},\qquad
v_{aj}=\omega_j z_{aj},\qquad \omega_1+\omega_2=1.
\label{eq:normalization}
\end{equation}
Here $L_j$ and $H_j$ are the minimum and maximum reference benefits. They are computed
once, including the no-construction options, and then held fixed across all schedules.
A criterion with no variation is omitted rather than divided by zero. The preference
weights $\omega_j$ are explicit choices, not entropy estimates; equal weights are only
a possible reference scenario and are not assigned in this draft.

Let $v_j^+=\max_a v_{aj}$ and $v_j^-=\min_a v_{aj}$. Then
\begin{equation}
D_a^{\pm}=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{2}(v_{aj}-v_j^{\pm})^2},\qquad
U_{i,y}(c)=100\frac{D_a^-}{D_a^++D_a^-}.
\label{eq:topsis}
\end{equation}
If both criteria are constant, all options are tied. Otherwise, a higher score indicates
closer proximity to the ideal combination of service and revenue. TOPSIS can favor a
compromise, but its weights and scaling do not guarantee a balanced outcome.

For the problem's nine-district priority ranking, we propose a common comparison of
four additional ports in each district in 2024. This holds the capacity increment fixed
while measuring local benefit; it is a diagnostic comparison, not a candidate-site
construction decision. Include these reference options when fixing the normalization
bounds. Actual schedules use each site's listed port count. District scores and rankings
remain unreported until the inputs are calibrated.

\section{Construction Scheduling by Enumeration}
\subsection{Schedule and Feasibility}
\label{sec:schedule}
The ten candidates in Table~\ref{tab:sites} define the search space. The three phases
are 2025--2026, 2027--2028 and 2029--2030, as specified in the problem.
\textbf{Assumption: full site capacity.} A selected site opens at the start of its phase
with its listed maximum number of ports and remains available afterward. The problem
gives site costs rather than a cost curve for partial installation, so this keeps the
construction choices directly tied to the supplied data.

Assign each site $z_s\in\{0,1,2,3\}$, where zero means never built. If $t(y)$ denotes
the phase containing year $y$, the available local capacity is
\begin{equation}
c_i(y;z)=c_i^0+\sum_{s\text{ in }i}M_s\,
\mathbf 1\{1\leq z_s\leq t(y)\}.
\label{eq:installed-capacity}
\end{equation}
Budget and initial station-count restrictions are
\begin{equation}
\sum_{s:z_s=t}C_s\leq B_t\quad(t=1,2,3),\qquad
4\leq\sum_s\mathbf 1\{z_s=1\}\leq6.
\label{eq:construction-constraints}
\end{equation}
In dollars, $B_1=2{,}000{,}000$. The problem does not supply Phase-2 or Phase-3 budgets;
$B_2$ and $B_3$ remain planning inputs. The base draft treats listed candidates as
buildable at the stated costs. A land score alone is not a verified exclusion threshold;
any additional feasibility restriction must be stated before the search.

\textbf{Interpretation: coverage by the final phase.} Selecting four to six stations
cannot simultaneously place a new station in each of nine districts. We therefore
interpret the latter requirement as a completed-rollout condition:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{s\text{ in }i}\mathbf 1\{z_s>0\}\geq1\quad\text{for each district }i.
\label{eq:terminal-coverage}
\end{equation}
This is an explicit resolution of conflicting instructions in the problem, not evidence
that four to six stations can cover nine districts under local-only charging.
The five-mile distance rule is reserved for the requested connectivity graph; it does
not transfer charging demand between districts.

\subsection{Utility and Exhaustive Search}
\label{sec:search}
We score the installed configuration in each year, so an early station continues to
contribute in later years. For declared time weights $\beta_y>0$, maximize
\begin{equation}
F(z)=\sum_{y=2025}^{2030}\beta_y\sum_{i=1}^{9}
\left[U_{i,y}\bigl(c_i(y;z)\bigr)-U_{i,y}(c_i^0)\right].
\label{eq:schedule-objective}
\end{equation}
Subtracting each no-construction score makes its incremental contribution zero. Equal
$\beta_y$ values would value all years equally; any discount must be chosen explicitly.
This is a planning utility index, not monetary lifetime profit. Independent districts
allow their utilities to be added. Within a district we evaluate combined capacity:
Downtown has four configurations (neither, S1, S2, both), so the same waiting reduction
is not credited twice to separate sites.

There are at most $4^{10}=1{,}048{,}576$ schedules. We use the following procedure:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Simulate each district, year and attainable local configuration once per random
      replicate. Store waiting, served visits, revenue and TOPSIS utility in a lookup table.
\item Enumerate phase assignments and reject those violating budget, station-count,
      coverage or declared feasibility constraints.
\item Retrieve each surviving schedule's annual local utilities and compute $F(z)$.
\item Keep the largest value; break exact ties by lower total installation cost, then by
      a fixed site order for reproducibility.
\end{enumerate}
All periods are chosen jointly; there is no separate optimization that freezes Phase 1.
With two candidates in Downtown and one in each other district, only 20 local
configurations per year are needed before adding the diagnostic ranking options.
The expensive queue simulations are therefore reused across schedules. Exhaustive search
finds the best feasible schedule for the chosen inputs and simulated objective, without
requiring a mixed-integer programming solver.

\paragraph{Inputs required before numerical results.}
Public-visit frequency, venue-to-profile mapping, peak windows and shares, occupation
durations, patience, session prices, TOPSIS weights, time weights and later-phase budgets
remain to be specified. This draft therefore stops at the evaluation and search method;
it does not reuse the former site's ranking or deployment schedule as a new result.
