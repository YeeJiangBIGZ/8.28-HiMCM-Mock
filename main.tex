In bout five years, the number of electric vehicles on Springfield's roads has grown nearly
sevenfold, from 320 to 2,180. Yet the city's public charging network has not kept pace.
One district, Riverside, has 120 electric cars and not a single public charger. Another,
Downtown, has 420 electric cars competing for just 12 chargers. This is the gap our team
was hired to close. GreenCharge asked us three questions: where is the demand greatest,
where should the first stations go, and how should the network grow over the next five
years.

We answer all three questions with a single framework built from the city's own data. To
measure demand, we score each of the nine districts on six factors. These factors are
population, number of electric vehicles, vehicle density, daily traffic volume, shortage of
existing public chargers, and district type. We assign each factor a weight through an
objective method that gives more weight to factors on which districts differ. This method
needs no subjective judgment. Each district then receives a priority score between 0 and
100.

To select the first stations, we model the decision as a facility-location problem. A driver
charges at the nearest station, so what matters is how far each district must travel to reach
one. We place stations to minimize the total travel distance, weighted by how many
drivers each district holds. We evaluate every valid combination of four to six sites within
a two-million-dollar budget and choose the set with the smallest weighted distance. The
optimal set places six stations in six districts and spends \$1.93 million.

To plan the future, we forecast electric-vehicle growth with a time-series model. This
model extends the recent growth trend rather than imposing a fixed curve, and it projects
about 3,200 vehicles by 2025 and 11,900 by 2030. We combine this forecast with a
break-even rule that signals when the installed chargers approach capacity. This rule
yields a three-phase roadmap that expands the network from six stations to ten, covering
all nine districts by 2030.

Finally, we test how the plan depends on our assumptions. If the growth forecast is off by
20\% in either direction, the phases shift by about one year, but the order of construction
does not change. If construction costs rise by 15\%, we build five stations rather than six
in the first phase. We deliver our findings as a plain-language report to the Mayor. The
report gives one clear recommendation. We advise the city to begin with the six-station
core in Downtown, Northside, Southside, Eastside, Westside, and Industrial, and to treat
the absence of any charger in Riverside as the single most valuable opportunity in the
city.

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Background and Real-World Motivation}

Transportation is the largest source of greenhouse gas emissions in most American cities,
and the fastest available lever for cutting those emissions is electrification. Springfield
has committed to a 50\% reduction in carbon emissions by 2030, and converting its
vehicle fleet to electricity is the stated centerpiece of that plan. The commitment is
already visible in the data: the city's electric-vehicle (EV) registrations grew from 320
in 2019 to 2,180 in 2024, a nearly sevenfold increase over five years.

The public charging network, however, has not grown in step. Across the nine districts,
existing public chargers number in the single digits, and one district, Riverside, has
none at all. This mismatch matters because public charging is a prerequisite for adoption.
Drivers who cannot charge at home, such as apartment residents, commuters, and commercial
fleets, postpone or abandon EV purchases when public chargers are scarce. The deployment of
charging stations is not a passive response to demand; it is a driver of demand.
Getting the placement right accelerates the city's climate goals. Getting it wrong strands
investment in underused locations.

The Mayor's Office has asked our team to design this network deliberately. The task is
inherently quantitative: demand is unevenly distributed across districts, candidate sites
differ in cost and capacity, and the network must grow over time as adoption spreads.
These features make the problem a natural fit for formal optimization and evaluation
methods.

\subsection{Problem Restatement}

We address four connected tasks. (1) Assess where current charging demand is concentrated
by identifying the factors that drive demand and ranking the nine districts by a computed
demand-priority score. (2) Select the optimal first-phase set of four to six charging
stations from ten candidate sites under a two-million-dollar installation budget, so that
the demand-weighted travel distance to the nearest station is minimized.
(3) Analyze the connectivity of the district
network and use the results, together with a forecast of EV growth, to build a phased
deployment roadmap from 2025 to 2030. (4) Deliver a plain-language recommendation report
to the Mayor and City Council.

Each task fixes the method we use: entropy-weight scoring with TOPSIS for demand, a
$p$-median model for station location, ARIMA for the EV forecast, and a break-even rule with
a multi-period integer program for expansion timing. These four methods connect the tasks
into one framework.

\subsection{Our Work and Overall Framework}

Our solution proceeds in three stages, mirroring the three modeling tasks.

\textbf{Stage 1 (Data and Demand Assessment)} screens the raw data, builds a six-indicator
evaluation system, and converts it to a demand-priority score on a 0--100 scale with
entropy weights and TOPSIS, then forecasts EV registrations with ARIMA through 2030.

\textbf{Stage 2 (Core Optimization)} models station placement as a $p$-median
facility-location problem, minimizing demand-weighted distance under budget, land, capacity,
and coverage constraints, solved exactly by enumeration. A district connectivity graph
explains why central districts are favored.

\textbf{Stage 3 (Expansion and Recommendation)} fixes the Phase-1 layout, times later phases
with a break-even rule, and schedules the remaining sites with a discounted multi-period
integer program. The resulting roadmap is tested against growth, cost, and budget
perturbations and distilled into a letter to the Mayor.

\section{Assumptions and Justification}

We state our modeling assumptions explicitly and justify each one.

\subsection{Demand and Forecasting Assumptions}

\textbf{Assumption 1:} Public charging demand is driven primarily by population, EV
density, traffic volume, existing charger scarcity, and district type.
\textit{Justification:} These are the factors the problem statement names, and they align
with empirical studies of charging-station utilization, which consistently find that
usage tracks local EV ownership, passing traffic, and the absence of home-charging
alternatives \cite{DOE-AFDC, NREL2017}. Commercial and administrative districts generate
additional daytime demand from commuters and visitors, captured by the district-type
factor.

\textbf{Assumption 2:} Citywide EV adoption can be forecast from its own six-year history
with an ARIMA time-series model, and it cannot exceed a long-run ceiling of 25,000
vehicles.
\textit{Justification:} ARIMA lets the recent data set the trajectory rather than imposing
a fixed shape, which is the natural choice when the only reliable predictor is the series'
own past \cite{Box2015, Hyndman2018}. A pure ARIMA has no saturation ceiling and grows
without bound, so we impose 25,000 vehicles as a conservative business bound. The bound sits
well above the 2030 forecast of 11,915 vehicles, so it caps only the distant tail and leaves
every phase-trigger year unchanged; Section 6.4 sweeps the ceiling over 15,000--35,000 and
confirms this invariance.

\subsection{Spatial and Feasibility Assumptions}

\textbf{Assumption 3:} The candidate sites listed in Table \ref{tab:sites} are the only
feasible locations, and their reported costs and capacities are accurate.
\textit{Justification:} The problem supplies these as the decision set. Site-specific
engineering constraints (electrical service, land) are already reflected in the
land-availability and maximum-capacity fields.

\textbf{Assumption 4:} A station serves demand in its own district and, to a lesser
degree, nearby districts, with proximity captured by the distance matrix.
\textit{Justification:} Drivers strongly prefer the nearest available charger. This is
the reason the location model minimizes travel distance to the nearest station rather than
ranking sites by abstract priority scores. The distance matrix $d_{ij}$ (Table
\ref{tab:notation}) supplies these distances, and the connectivity graph built from it is
used to explain why central districts are favored.

\textbf{Assumption 5:} The district distance matrix is symmetric and represents driving
distance between district centroids.
\textit{Justification:} The supplied matrix is symmetric, consistent with road-network
travel between district centers. We use it both for the connectivity graph (Task 2-c) and
for the $p$-median location model, whose objective is this distance.

\textbf{Assumption 6:} The requirement to place ``at least one station in each of the nine
districts'' is a terminal coverage goal, not a constraint on the first-phase selection.
\textit{Justification:} The problem asks us to select four to six stations in Phase 1 while
simultaneously requiring at least one station in each of nine districts. These
requirements cannot hold together, because nine districts require at least nine stations.
We resolve the conflict by treating per-district coverage as the goal of the complete
three-phase rollout (2025--2030), achieved by Phase 3. Phase 1 optimizes over four to six
sites under the budget alone.

\subsection{Economic and Financial Assumptions}

\textbf{Assumption 7:} Each EV generates about \$50 per year in charging revenue, station
installation is amortized over a ten-year horizon, a public charger serves roughly 75 EVs at
capacity, and each new station is built to its listed maximum charger capacity.
\textit{Justification:} These values anchor the break-even analysis in Section 6. The
revenue figure is a conservative industry average within the \$35--\$65 range reported in
public-charging studies \cite{NREL2017, Funke2019, IEA2024}; the 75-EV-per-charger rule sits in
the 60--90 range observed across comparable cities; and the ten-year amortization is a
standard engineering-economics convention. Section 6.4 sweeps the revenue and utilization
figures and shows the phase timing is insensitive to both. Building each station to its
listed maximum capacity fixes the Phase-1 total at 40 chargers.

\section{Task 1: Charging Demand Assessment}

\subsection{Data Exploration and Preprocessing}

Before building any model we examine the raw dataset for quality and structure, confirming
that the data support the assumptions that follow and flagging values that would distort
the analysis.

\textbf{Missing values.} Every cell of the district indicator matrix and candidate-site
table is present, so no imputation is needed.

\textbf{Outlier detection.} For each indicator we compute standardized Z-scores and flag any
district with $|Z|>2.5$. Exactly one qualifies: Downtown's EV density ($Z=+2.52$, a 4.94\%
concentration) sits more than two standard deviations above the mean. This is a genuine
feature, not an error, and it is why Downtown tops the demand ranking.

\textbf{Correlation analysis.} We compute the pairwise correlation matrix and flag
coefficients with magnitude above $0.8$ (Figure \ref{fig:correlation}). Four pairs exceed
the threshold, led by daily traffic with district type ($r=0.92$). The entropy-weight
method handles this redundancy by down-weighting correlated indicators.

\subsection{Factors Influencing Charging Demand}

We identify five primary drivers of public charging demand, each of which enters our
evaluation model as a measurable indicator.

\textbf{Population.} A larger resident base supports more vehicles and more potential EV
owners, raising baseline charging need.

\textbf{EV density.} Many EVs per resident produce an immediate, concentrated need for
charging, whereas the same number spread thinly produces lower pressure on any location.

\textbf{Traffic volume.} Daily traffic captures through-flow and visitor activity that
resident population misses. A busy commercial corridor needs charging for non-residents
even if few EVs are registered locally, which is why Mall District is a strong demand
center despite its small population.

\textbf{Existing charger scarcity.} Demand for \emph{new} public charging is highest where
supply is thinnest, measured as existing chargers per thousand residents.

\textbf{District type.} Commercial, administrative, and educational districts generate
daytime and visitor demand that residential districts do not, so we map each type to a
propensity score.

\subsection{Entropy-Weight Method}

Assigning equal weight to six indicators would ignore that districts differ sharply on
some dimensions and barely on others. An indicator on which all districts score similarly
provides little information for ranking. We use the entropy-weight method to derive
objective weights from the data \cite{Shannon1948}.

Let $X = [x_{ij}]_{m \times n}$ be the decision matrix with $m = 9$ districts and $n = 6$
indicators. We first normalize to remove scale differences. For benefit-type indicators
(direction $+$):

\begin{equation}
r_{ij} = \frac{x_{ij} - \min_i x_{ij}}{\max_i x_{ij} - \min_i x_{ij}}
\end{equation}

For cost-type indicators (direction $-$), we use the mirrored formula:

\begin{equation}
r_{ij} = \frac{\max_i x_{ij} - x_{ij}}{\max_i x_{ij} - \min_i x_{ij}}
\end{equation}

The normalized matrix $R = [r_{ij}]$ has entries in $[0,1]$ with larger values indicating
higher demand priority. The entropy of indicator $j$ is

\begin{equation}
p_{ij} = \frac{r_{ij}}{\sum_{i=1}^m r_{ij}}, \qquad
e_j = -\frac{1}{\ln m}\sum_{i=1}^m p_{ij}\ln p_{ij}
\end{equation}

with $p_{ij}\ln p_{ij}=0$ when $p_{ij}=0$. High entropy means districts score similarly on
that indicator, so it carries little discriminating information. The objective weight is

\begin{equation}
w_j = \frac{1 - e_j}{\sum_{k=1}^n (1 - e_k)}
\end{equation}

The weights sum to one and reflect each indicator's ability to separate districts.
Table \ref{tab:weights} reports the computed weights.

\subsection{TOPSIS Ranking and Demand Priority Scores}

TOPSIS ranks districts by their geometric distance from an ideal and a negative-ideal
solution \cite{Hwang1981, Zhang2011}. We form the weighted matrix $V = [v_{ij}]$ with
$v_{ij} = w_j r_{ij}$, and define the ideal $A^+$ and negative-ideal $A^-$ solutions:

\begin{equation}
A^+ = \{\max_i v_{i1}, \dots, \max_i v_{in}\}, \qquad
A^- = \{\min_i v_{i1}, \dots, \min_i v_{in}\}
\end{equation}

For each district $i$, the Euclidean distances to these references are

\begin{equation}
D_i^+ = \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^n (v_{ij} - A_j^+)^2}, \qquad
D_i^- = \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^n (v_{ij} - A_j^-)^2}
\end{equation}

The relative closeness to the ideal defines the demand priority score, rescaled to 0--100:

\begin{equation}
S_i = \frac{D_i^-}{D_i^+ + D_i^-} \times 100
\end{equation}

\subsection{Citywide EV Registration Forecast}
\label{sec:forecast}

To plan the expansion of Springfield's charging network, we must estimate how many electric vehicles the city will have in the coming years. The deployment roadmap in Task~3 depends on knowing when demand will exceed the capacity of the currently installed chargers. This section forecasts citywide EV registrations through 2030, with particular attention to 2025--2027 as required by Task~1c. \subsubsection{Data and Forecasting Setup}

The forecast horizon extends to 2030 to align with the city's carbon-reduction goal.

\subsection{ARIMA Model}
\label{subsec:arima}

The ARIMA model describes a time series using its own past values (autoregressive terms) and past forecast errors (moving-average terms). It does not impose a predetermined shape, allowing the recent growth pattern to determine the trajectory. We first test for stationarity using the Augmented Dickey--Fuller (ADF) test. The test fails to reject a unit root in both the level and the first difference, so we difference the series twice and set the integration order $d = 2$. Let $C_t$ denote citywide registrations in year $t$, and let $\nabla^2 C_t = C_t - 2C_{t-1} + C_{t-2}$ be its second difference. The general ARIMA$(p,d,q)$ model is

\begin{equation}
\nabla^d C_t = \phi_1 \nabla^d C_{t-1} + \cdots + \phi_p \nabla^d C_{t-p} + \epsilon_t + \theta_1 \epsilon_{t-1} + \cdots + \theta_q \epsilon_{t-q},
\label{eq:arima}
\end{equation}

where $\epsilon_t$ is white noise. We select $p, q \in \{0,1\}$ by minimizing the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). The chosen model is ARIMA$(1,2,0)$ with AIC $= 52.07$, autoregressive coefficient $\hat{\phi}_1 = 0.935$, and residual standard deviation $79$ vehicles.

Figure~\ref{fig:acf-pacf} shows the second-differenced series alongside its ACF and PACF. Because the second-differenced series contains only four observations, partial autocorrelations can be estimated at lag~1 only. Both lag-1 values fall within the 95\% significance bounds, which is consistent with the ARIMA$(1,2,0)$ specification though not strong evidence for it; Section~\ref{subsec:limitations} notes the limited power of diagnostics on such a short series.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{ev_arima_acf_pacf.png}
\caption{Second-differenced EV registration series (left) with its autocorrelation function (center) and partial autocorrelation function (right). With only four second-differenced observations, lag-1 values are the only estimable ones.}
\label{fig:acf-pacf}
\end{figure}

A pure ARIMA model has no saturation ceiling and would grow without bound over a long horizon. We therefore impose a conservative business upper bound of 25{,}000 vehicles, well above the 2030 forecast, so that it caps only the distant tail and does not affect the near-term projections. The forecast with this cap is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ev-forecast}, along with the historical data and the logistic baseline.

\subsection{Logistic S-Curve Baseline}
\label{subsec:logistic}

The logistic model is the classic technology-diffusion curve, often used to describe the adoption of new products such as electric vehicles. It assumes that growth starts slowly, accelerates, and eventually saturates at a carrying capacity $K$. The model is

\begin{equation}
C_t = \frac{K}{1 + e^{-r(t - t_0)}},
\label{eq:logistic}
\end{equation}

where $r$ is the growth rate and $t_0$ is the inflection point. We fit this curve to the same 2019--2024 data. Because the logistic model forces an S-shape, it provides a useful upper-bound scenario: if adoption accelerates faster than the ARIMA trend, the logistic forecast represents a more optimistic future. We do not adopt it as the primary forecast because the short data series does not yet show clear evidence of saturation, and the imposed shape may overstate long-run growth.

\subsection{Model Selection and Adopted Forecast}
\label{subsec:forecast-selection}

We adopt the ARIMA forecast as the primary input for the expansion plan. This choice is based on three considerations. First, the ARIMA residuals satisfy the assumptions of statistical inference, whereas the logistic curve imposes a saturation shape that is not yet supported by the data. Second, the ARIMA model follows the recent growth trend without forcing an early inflection, making it more conservative for public investment. Third, the near-term forecasts (2025--2027) are similar across both models, so the Phase-1 and Phase-2 decisions are robust to this choice. The logistic curve is retained as an optimistic upper scenario for the sensitivity analysis in Section~\ref{subsec:sensitivity}.

\subsection{Implications for Deployment Planning}
\label{subsec:forecast-implications}

The forecast provides the demand input for the phased deployment roadmap in Task~3. Using an empirical utilization rule of 75 EVs per public charger, we compute the citywide EV thresholds that trigger each expansion phase: Phase~2 begins when registrations reach 5{,}475, and Phase~3 begins at 6{,}975. Under the adopted ARIMA forecast, these thresholds are crossed in 2027 and 2028, respectively. This timing supports a three-phase buildout: six stations in 2025--2026, two additional stations in 2027--2028, and the final two stations in 2029--2030. The full break-even calculation and the multi-period optimization are presented in Section~\ref{sec:expansion}. The forecast thus confirms that Springfield's EV growth justifies a staged, demand-driven expansion rather than a single large construction program.

\section{Task 2: Charging Station Location Optimization}

\subsection{Site Attributes and the Facility-Location Objective}

A charging station is useful only if drivers can reach it. This one fact fixes the
objective of our location model. A driver who needs a charge travels to the nearest open
station, so the quantity that matters is the distance from each district to its closest
station, not a sum of priority scores attached to individual sites. We weight that
distance by the district's demand, because a mile of extra travel matters more in a
district with many electric cars than in one with few.

Each district $i$ carries a demand weight $w_i = S_i / 100$, the TOPSIS score of
Section 4 rescaled to the unit interval. A station in district $j$ serves every district
$i$ within the five-mile coverage radius, the same five-mile threshold the problem uses for
the connectivity task of Section 5.5, so the coverage constraint and the connectivity
analysis share one distance standard. The cost of a layout is the sum, over districts,
of $w_i$ times the distance to district $i$'s nearest station. This is the
demand-weighted travel distance, and it is what the model minimizes.

Two feasibility filters carry over from the site data. A site must be developable and must
be able to absorb the demand it serves:

\begin{equation}
L_s \ge 3 \qquad \text{(land availability at least 3 of 5)},
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
M_s \ge 4 \qquad \text{(capacity of at least 4 chargers)}.
\end{equation}

The land filter excludes S9 (Mall District), whose land score is 2, from the Phase-1
candidate set; every other site passes. The capacity filter is non-binding, because all ten
sites host at least four chargers. Table \ref{tab:sites} lists the site-level data.

\subsection{$p$-Median Facility-Location Formulation}

We formalize the objective as a $p$-median facility-location problem
\cite{Daskin1995}. Let $y_j\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether site $j$ is opened, and let
$x_{ij}\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether district $i$ is assigned to the station at site $j$.
The model chooses open sites and assigns every district to one open site so as to minimize
the demand-weighted travel distance:

\begin{equation}
\min \sum_{i=1}^{9}\sum_{j=1}^{10} w_i\, d_{ij}\, x_{ij}
\end{equation}

subject to

\begin{equation}
\sum_{j=1}^{10} x_{ij} = 1, \qquad i=1,\dots,9 \qquad \text{(every district is served)}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
x_{ij} \le y_j, \qquad i=1,\dots,9,\; j=1,\dots,10 \qquad \text{(only open stations serve)}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
x_{ij} = 0 \;\; \text{whenever } d_{ij} > 5 \qquad \text{(coverage radius)}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\sum_{j=1}^{10} c_j\, y_j \le 200 \qquad \text{(budget: \$2,000,000)}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
4 \le \sum_{j=1}^{10} y_j \le 6 \qquad \text{(station count)}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
L_j \ge 3 y_j, \qquad M_j \ge 4 y_j \qquad \text{(land and capacity filters)}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
y_j,\; x_{ij} \in \{0,1\}.
\end{equation}

The first three constraints define the assignment. The coverage radius forces $x_{ij}=0$
when district $i$ lies more than five miles from site $j$, so no district is assigned to a
station it cannot reach. Because drivers always choose the nearest station, in an optimal
solution district $i$ is assigned to the closest open site, so the assignment variables
$x_{ij}$ are determined once the open sites $y_j$ are chosen. We can solve the
model by enumerating the open sets directly.

Why $p$-median rather than maximal covering? A maximal-covering model asks only whether a
district lies within the radius and ignores the distance inside it. With a five-mile radius
and nine compact districts, a few central stations already cover every district, so maximal
covering stops discriminating after three stations. The $p$-median objective keeps
rewarding smaller distances after coverage is met, so it ranks layouts precisely. We
minimize weighted distance rather than maximize coverage.

With ten candidate sites the feasible sets number at most
$\binom{10}{4}+\binom{10}{5}+\binom{10}{6}=672$, so we enumerate every feasible
combination, evaluate its demand-weighted distance, and keep the smallest, breaking ties by
lower cost. The result is provably optimal.

\subsection{Break-Even Analysis for Phase Timing}

The Phase-1 selection answers where to build first. To decide \emph{when} to build the next
stations we need a data-driven trigger rather than an arbitrary EV threshold. We derive the
trigger from a break-even calculation for each site.

A station breaks even when the revenue it collects from the EVs it serves covers its
annualized cost. Let $c_s$ be the installation cost and $m_s$ the annual maintenance cost
of site $s$. Amortizing installation over a ten-year horizon at $\alpha=0.1$, and assuming
each EV contributes $R=\$50$ per year in charging revenue, the break-even number of EVs is

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{BE}_s = \frac{\alpha\, c_s + m_s}{R} = \frac{0.1\, c_s + m_s}{50}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Multi-Period Integer Program}

The $p$-median model answers \emph{where} to build first. To answer \emph{what} to build
next, and in which year, we fix the Phase-1 layout from Section 5 and schedule the
remaining sites with a discounted integer program. Let $x_{s,t}\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether
the remaining site $s$ is opened in phase $t\in\{2,3\}$. A site opened earlier serves
demand sooner, so we discount phase-$t$ demand by $\delta^{t}$ with $\delta=0.95$:

\begin{equation}
\max \sum_{t=2}^{3}\sum_{s\notin P_1} \delta^{t}\, q_s\, x_{s,t}
\end{equation}

subject to

\begin{equation}
\sum_{t=2}^{3} x_{s,t} \le 1 \;\; \forall s \qquad \text{(each site built at most once)}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\sum_{s\notin P_1} x_{s,t} = 2, \qquad t = 2, 3 \qquad \text{(two stations per phase)}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\sum_{s\notin P_1} c_s\, x_{s,t} \le B_t, \qquad (B_2, B_3) = (90, 90) \times \$10{,}000
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
x_{s,t} \in \{0,1\}.
\end{equation}

Here $P_1$ is the fixed Phase-1 set and $q_s$ is the \emph{incremental} demand of site
$s$: the district's demand weight if the district has no station yet, and zero if it does.
A second station in an already-served district (S1, a second Downtown site) adds no new
coverage, so it carries $q_s = 0$ and is deferred to the last phase as a capacity site. The
objective ranks the remaining coverage districts by discounted demand. Phase 2
opens the two highest, Mall District (0.5030) and University (0.4680), and Phase 3
completes coverage with Riverside (0.2611) plus the Downtown capacity site. Table
\ref{tab:multiperiod} reports the schedule, together with the demand-weighted distance left
after each phase.

\subsection{Three-Phase Deployment Roadmap}

The multi-period optimum, combined with the break-even crossing years, produces the
following roadmap.

\textbf{Phase 1 (2025--2026): six stations.} Build S2 (Downtown), S3 (Northside), S4
(Southside), S5 (Eastside), S6 (Westside), and S8 (Industrial), one station per district at
\$1.93 million, matching the $p$-median optimum of Section 5.

\textbf{Phase 2 (2027--2028): two additional stations.} Add S7 (University) and S9 (Mall
District), whose districts carry the highest remaining demand (0.4680 and 0.5030). The
break-even rule triggers Phase 2 when citywide EVs cross 5,475, in 2027. University's high
degree makes it an efficient second-wave hub.

\textbf{Phase 3 (2029--2030): full coverage and capacity.} Add S10 (Riverside) when
citywide EVs cross 6,975 (2028), closing the city's only zero-charger district, and add S1
as a second Downtown station to absorb the district's growing fleet. By 2030 the forecast
reaches about 11,915 EVs, so even low-current-demand districts need local access.

The roadmap is demand-driven at every step: the order of districts follows the demand
ranking through the discounted objective, and the phase timing follows the break-even
crossing years.

\subsection{Sensitivity Analysis}

We test how the plan responds to four perturbations: EV growth error, installation-cost
change, budget reduction, and forced station requirements.

\textbf{EV growth $\pm$20\%.} We scale the forecast by 0.8 and 1.2 and re-examine the
phase timing (Figure \ref{fig:sensitivity}, left). A 20\% error shifts the break-even
crossing years by at most one year. The Phase-2 trigger (5,475 EVs) moves between 2027 and
2028, and the Phase-3 trigger (6,975 EVs) between 2028 and 2029. The \emph{order} of
district build-out is unchanged, because the demand weights feeding the $p$-median objective
depend on relative differences that are stable under uniform scaling. The plan is robust in
structure and only mildly sensitive in timing.

\textbf{Installation cost $\pm$15\%.} We re-solve the $p$-median program with costs scaled
by 0.85 and 1.15 (Table \ref{tab:costsens}). At $-$15\% the optimum is unchanged: the six

$+$15\% the budget binds, so the model drops S8 (Industrial), the station with the smallest
marginal distance saving, and settles on five stations at 4.59 miles. The plan is more
sensitive to cost increases than to decreases because the base plan already sits near the
budget ceiling.

\section{Model Evaluation}

\subsection{Strengths}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Objective indicator weighting.} The entropy-weight method derives
    indicator importance from the data rather than expert judgment, making the demand
    ranking reproducible and free of subjective bias.
    \item \textbf{Provably optimal location selection.} Because the candidate set is small,
    both the Phase-1 and multi-period programs are solved by complete enumeration. The
    reported selections are globally optimal, not heuristic guesses.
    \item \textbf{One interpretable objective.} The $p$-median model minimizes a single
    quantity, the demand-weighted travel distance, which has a direct physical meaning. Every
    chosen site is justified by the distance it saves for high-demand districts. Centrality
    and cost enter only as diagnostics and as the budget constraint, not as hidden
    trade-off coefficients.
    \item \textbf{Data-driven phase timing.} The break-even rule ties each build phase to a
    computed utilization threshold rather than an arbitrary EV number, and the multi-period
    program optimizes all three phases jointly.
    \item \textbf{Computational efficiency.} The entire pipeline executes in a fraction of
    a second, allowing interactive what-if analysis by city planners.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations and Improvement Schemes}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Short sample and imposed ceiling.} The ARIMA forecast is fit on only six
    annual observations, so the ADF test has little power and the chosen order is sensitive
    to the small sample. The 25,000 ceiling is a business judgment imposed after fitting,
    not a fitted parameter. Sweeping the ceiling over 15,000--35,000 in Section 6.4 leaves
    the near-term forecast unchanged, and over 2025--2027 the forecast stays below both the
    ceiling and the logistic alternative, so the near-term roadmap timing is stable.
    \item \textbf{Qualitative district-type mapping.} The district-type propensity is a
    judgment-based 0--1 scale, though it is justified by the infrastructure literature cited
    in Section 4. The sweep of Section 6.4 shows the Phase-1 selection is invariant to
    uniform rescaling, because min-max normalization removes scale. What the ranking still
    depends on is the relative ordering of district types, which could be replaced with
    measured proxies such as employment density or point-of-interest counts.
    \item \textbf{Enumeration does not scale.} The multi-period program enumerates all
    $4^{10}$ phase assignments. This is exact for ten sites but would not scale to a city
    with many more candidates or phases; a branch-and-bound or column-generation method
    would be needed for larger instances.
    \item \textbf{No queuing or utilization modeling.} We do not model waiting times,
    charger utilization rates, or station saturation. A queuing-based extension would
    refine capacity decisions where demand is densest.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusions}

\subsection{Summary of Results}

We developed a quantitative framework for deploying Springfield's public EV charging
network. Our main contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A demand assessment model} that scores nine districts on six indicators
    using entropy weights and TOPSIS. Downtown ranks first (72.24) and Riverside last
    (26.11), with a clear, explainable ranking in between.
    \item \textbf{A $p$-median facility-location model} that selects six Phase-1 stations
    (S2, S3, S4, S5, S6, S8), one in each of six districts, at \$1.93 million, minimizing the
    demand-weighted travel distance over every feasible six-site combination.
    \item \textbf{A connectivity and distance analysis} showing the district network is
    fully connected, with Downtown and University as the highest-degree hubs, and that
    centrality alone does not override travel distance.
    \item \textbf{An ARIMA EV forecast} projecting 3,242 vehicles in 2025, 6,083 in 2027,
    and 11,915 in 2030, driving a demand-ordered roadmap.
    \item \textbf{A phased deployment plan} solved by a discounted multi-period integer
    program, growing from 6 to 8 to 10 stations, with each phase timed by a break-even
    threshold, achieving full nine-district coverage by 2030.
    \item \textbf{A data-exploration and break-even methodology} that screens the raw data
    for missing values, outliers, and collinearity, and derives phase triggers from station
    economics rather than arbitrary thresholds.
    \item \textbf{A robustness analysis} showing the plan's structure is stable under
    $\pm$20\% growth error and budget cuts, with only the marginal station at risk when
    costs rise.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Future Work and Deployment}

The framework transfers to any city that shares Springfield's data. The improvement paths
named in Section 7.2, a branch-and-bound solver and a queuing model, would carry it to
larger networks.

\section*{Appendix: Python Implementation}

\textbf{Dependencies:} Python 3.13+, NumPy 2.5+, SciPy 1.18+, Matplotlib 3.11+.
Complete source code: \texttt{ev\_charging\_model.py} (supplementary material).

\vspace{4pt}

\noindent\textbf{Core Module Architecture.} The codebase implements the deployment model
in eight modules, each reproducing a numbered result in this paper.

\texttt{data\_exploration()} checks for missing values, computes indicator Z-scores to flag
outliers, and builds the correlation matrix. It produces Figure \ref{fig:correlation}.

\texttt{demand\_model()} normalizes the six-indicator decision matrix with
direction-dependent min-max formulas, computes indicator entropies, and returns the
entropy weights and TOPSIS demand scores. This function produces Tables
\ref{tab:weights} and \ref{tab:scores}.

\texttt{ev\_forecast\_arima()} runs the ADF stationarity test, selects the ARIMA order by
minimum AIC, and forecasts registrations through 2030 with a 25,000 cap. The companion
\texttt{ev\_forecast()} keeps the logistic S-curve as a comparison baseline, and
\texttt{compare\_forecasts()} prints the side-by-side table. Together they produce Table
\ref{tab:forecast} and Figures \ref{fig:forecast} and \ref{fig:acfpacf}.

\texttt{location\_model()} attaches each district's demand weight, degree centrality, and
installation cost to its candidate sites, then solves the $p$-median facility-location
program (minimize the demand-weighted distance) by exhaustive enumeration over all
combinations of four to six sites under the budget, land, capacity, and coverage
constraints. The companion \texttt{baseline\_comparison()} evaluates greedy, population,
and random placement rules against the optimum. Together they produce Tables
\ref{tab:sites} and \ref{tab:phase1} and Figure \ref{fig:placement}.

\texttt{graph\_model()} constructs the undirected distance-threshold graph (edges at
$\leq 5$ miles), computes node degrees via the adjacency matrix, and finds connected
components by breadth-first search. It produces Table \ref{tab:degrees}.

\texttt{break\_even()} computes each site's break-even EV count and the citywide Phase-2
and Phase-3 thresholds. It produces Table \ref{tab:breakeven}.

\texttt{multi\_period()} solves the discounted three-phase integer program over all
$4^{10}$ phase assignments. It produces Table \ref{tab:multiperiod}.

\texttt{sensitivity()} re-solves the $p$-median program under scaled growth, scaled costs,
reduced budgets, and forced-station constraints, then sweeps the business ceiling, the
utilization ratio, the per-EV revenue, and the district-type scale. It produces Figure
\ref{fig:sensitivity} and Tables \ref{tab:costsens}, \ref{tab:budget}, and \ref{tab:whatif},
together with Figure \ref{fig:sweep} and Table \ref{tab:sweep}.

All experiments are deterministic or use a fixed random seed (seed 42 for the random
baseline), so every number is reproducible. Execute \texttt{python3 ev\_charging\_model.py}
to regenerate every figure and table in this paper.
