In five years, the number of electric vehicles on Springfield's roads has grown nearly
sevenfold, from 320 to 2,180. Yet the city's public charging network has not kept pace.
One district, Riverside, has 120 electric cars and not a single public charger. Another,
Downtown, has 420 electric cars competing for just 12 chargers. This is the gap our team
was hired to close. GreenCharge asked us three questions: where is the demand greatest,
where should the first stations go, and how should the network grow over the next five
years.

We answer all three with a single, transparent framework built from the city's own data.
To find demand, we score each of the nine districts on six factors: population, number of
electric vehicles, how concentrated those vehicles are, daily traffic volume, how few
public chargers already exist, and the type of district. We weight these factors
objectively, using a method that gives more importance to the factors on which districts
actually differ, rather than asking anyone for a subjective opinion. The result is a
priority score from 0 to 100 for every district.

To choose the first stations, we turn the decision into a precise optimization problem. We
rank the ten candidate sites by how much demand they serve, how central their location is,
and how much they cost to build. We then search every allowable combination of four to six
sites under a two-million-dollar budget, picking the set that serves the most demand first
and using centrality and cost only to settle ties. The winning set places six stations
across five districts, spends \$1.98 million, and reaches the five districts where drivers
need charging most urgently.

To plan the future, we forecast electric-vehicle growth with a time-series model that
extends the recent growth trend rather than imposing a fixed curve. The model projects
about 3,200 vehicles by 2025 and 11,900 by 2030. We use this forecast,
together with a break-even rule that signals when the chargers already built are nearing
capacity, to lay out a three-phase roadmap that grows the network from 6 stations to a
full 10, covering all nine districts by 2030.

Finally, we test how much the plan depends on our assumptions. If our growth forecast is
off by 20\% in either direction, the phases shift by roughly one year, but the order in
which we build does not change. If construction costs rise 15\%, we build five stations
instead of six in the first phase. We deliver our findings as a plain-language report to
the Mayor, with a clear recommendation: begin with the six-station core in Downtown,
Northside, Southside, Eastside, and Westside, and treat Riverside's zero-charger gap as
the single highest-leverage opportunity in the city.

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Background and Real-World Motivation}

Transportation is the largest source of greenhouse gas emissions in most American cities,
and the fastest available lever for cutting those emissions is electrification. Springfield
has committed to a 50\% reduction in carbon emissions by 2030, and converting its
vehicle fleet to electricity is the stated centerpiece of that plan. The commitment is
already visible in the data: the city's electric-vehicle (EV) registrations grew from 320
in 2019 to 2,180 in 2024, a nearly sevenfold increase over five years.

The public charging network, however, has not grown in step. Across the nine districts,
existing public chargers number in the single digits, and one district, Riverside, has
none at all. This mismatch matters because public charging is a prerequisite for adoption.
Drivers who cannot charge at home---apartment residents, commuters, and commercial fleets---
postpone or abandon EV purchases when public chargers are scarce. The deployment of
charging stations is therefore not a passive response to demand; it is a driver of demand.
Getting the placement right accelerates the city's climate goals. Getting it wrong strands
investment in underused locations.

The Mayor's Office has asked our team to design this network deliberately. The task is
inherently quantitative: demand is unevenly distributed across districts, candidate sites
differ in cost and capacity, and the network must grow over time as adoption spreads.
These features make the problem a natural fit for formal optimization and evaluation
methods.

\subsection{Problem Restatement}

We address four connected tasks. (1) Assess where current charging demand is concentrated
by identifying the factors that drive demand and ranking the nine districts by a computed
demand-priority score. (2) Select the optimal first-phase set of four to six charging
stations from ten candidate sites under a two-million-dollar installation budget, while
maximizing demand coverage and using network centrality and cost as tie-breakers.
(3) Analyze the connectivity of the district
network and use the results, together with a forecast of EV growth, to build a phased
deployment roadmap from 2025 to 2030. (4) Deliver a plain-language recommendation report
to the Mayor and City Council.

\subsection{Our Work and Overall Framework}

Our solution proceeds in three stages, mirroring the three modeling tasks.

\textbf{Stage 1 (Data and Demand Assessment)} first screens the raw data for missing
values, outliers, and collinear indicators, then builds a six-indicator evaluation system
for the nine districts. An entropy-weight method derives objective indicator weights from
the data itself, and TOPSIS converts each district's indicator profile into a
demand-priority score on a 0--100 scale. An ARIMA time-series model forecasts citywide EV
registrations through 2030.

\textbf{Stage 2 (Core Optimization)} attaches three quantities to each candidate site:
demand coverage, degree centrality, and installation cost. A lexicographic zero-one
integer program selects the set of four to six sites that maximizes demand first and uses
centrality and cost only to break ties, under the budget, land, capacity, and coverage
constraints, solved exactly by enumeration. We also construct the district connectivity
graph and compute node degrees.

\textbf{Stage 3 (Expansion and Recommendation)} derives a break-even rule to time each
build phase, then solves a discounted multi-period integer program that optimizes all
three phases together. The result is a phased deployment roadmap tested against
perturbations in growth, cost, and budget, distilled into a non-technical letter to the
Mayor.

\section{Assumptions and Justification}

We state our modeling assumptions explicitly and justify each one.

\textbf{Assumption 1:} Public charging demand is driven primarily by population, EV
density, traffic volume, existing charger scarcity, and district type.
\textit{Justification:} These are the factors the problem statement names, and they align
with empirical studies of charging-station utilization, which consistently find that
usage tracks local EV ownership, passing traffic, and the absence of home-charging
alternatives \cite{DOE-AFDC, NREL2017}. Commercial and administrative districts generate
additional daytime demand from commuters and visitors, captured by the district-type
factor.

\textbf{Assumption 2:} Citywide EV adoption can be forecast from its own six-year history
with an ARIMA time-series model, and it cannot exceed a long-run ceiling of roughly 25,000
vehicles.
\textit{Justification:} ARIMA lets the recent data set the trajectory rather than imposing
a fixed shape, which is the natural choice when the only reliable predictor is the series'
own past \cite{Box2015, Hyndman2018}. Springfield has roughly 48,000 households and 91,000
vehicles; a 25\% long-run EV share corresponds to about 23,000--25,000 vehicles, a
plausible saturation for a city committed to deep decarbonization \cite{Bass1969}. A pure
ARIMA has no such ceiling, so we impose the 25,000 upper bound explicitly to keep the
forecast from growing without limit.

\textbf{Assumption 3:} The candidate sites listed in Table 2 are the only feasible
locations, and their reported costs and capacities are accurate.
\textit{Justification:} The problem supplies these as the decision set. Site-specific
engineering constraints (electrical service, land) are already reflected in the
land-availability and maximum-capacity fields.

\textbf{Assumption 4:} A station serves demand in its own district and, to a lesser
degree, nearby districts, with proximity captured by the distance matrix.
\textit{Justification:} Drivers strongly prefer the nearest available charger. The
distance matrix in Table 3 allows us to measure each district's centrality within the
network, expressed as node degree and used as a tie-breaking objective in the
lexicographic location model.

\textbf{Assumption 5:} The district distance matrix is symmetric and represents driving
distance between district centroids.
\textit{Justification:} The supplied matrix is symmetric, consistent with road-network
travel between district centers. We use it both for the connectivity graph (Task 2-c) and
for the centrality component of the location model.

\textbf{Assumption 6:} The requirement to place ``at least one station in each of the nine
districts'' is a terminal coverage goal, not a constraint on the first-phase selection.
\textit{Justification:} The problem asks us to select four to six stations in Phase 1 while
simultaneously requiring at least one station in each of nine districts. These
requirements cannot hold together, because nine districts require at least nine stations.
We resolve the conflict by treating per-district coverage as the goal of the complete
three-phase rollout (2025--2030), achieved by Phase 3. Phase 1 optimizes over four to six
sites under the budget alone.

\textbf{Assumption 7:} Each EV generates about \$50 per year in charging revenue, station
installation is amortized over a ten-year horizon, and a public charger serves roughly 75
EVs at capacity.
\textit{Justification:} These values anchor the break-even analysis in Section 5 and come
from public-charging utilization studies \cite{NREL2017, Funke2019}. The revenue figure is
a conservative industry average, and the 75-EV-per-charger rule reflects observed
charger-to-vehicle ratios in comparable cities.

\section{Task 1: Charging Demand Assessment}

\subsection{Data Exploration and Preprocessing}

Before building any model we examine the raw dataset for quality and structure, confirming
that the data support the assumptions that follow and flagging values that would distort
the analysis.

\textbf{Missing values.} Every cell of the district indicator matrix and candidate-site
table is present, so no imputation is needed.

\textbf{Outlier detection.} For each indicator we compute standardized Z-scores and flag any
district with $|Z|>2.5$. Exactly one observation qualifies: Downtown's EV density
($Z=+2.52$), a concentration (4.94\%) more than two standard deviations above the mean,
driven by its small population and large EV count. This is a genuine feature, not a data
error, and it is why Downtown tops the demand ranking.

\textbf{Correlation analysis.} We compute the pairwise correlation matrix of the six
indicators and flag any coefficient with magnitude above $0.8$. Five pairs exceed the
threshold (Figure \ref{fig:correlation}). The strongest is daily traffic versus existing
chargers per thousand residents ($r=0.92$); daily traffic also correlates with district
type ($r=0.92$), and population correlates negatively with traffic ($r=-0.83$). These
correlations reflect the underlying structure of the city and are handled by the
entropy-weight method, which down-weights redundant information.

\subsection{Factors Influencing Charging Demand}

We identify four primary drivers of public charging demand, each of which enters our
evaluation model as a measurable indicator.

\textbf{Population.} A larger resident base supports more vehicles and more potential EV
owners, raising baseline charging need.

\textbf{EV density.} Many EVs per resident produce an immediate, concentrated need for
charging, whereas the same number spread thinly produces lower pressure on any location.

\textbf{Traffic volume.} Daily traffic captures through-flow and visitor activity that
resident population misses. A busy commercial corridor needs charging for non-residents
even if few EVs are registered locally, which is why Mall District is a strong demand
center despite its small population.

\textbf{Existing charger scarcity.} Demand for \emph{new} public charging is highest where
supply is thinnest, measured as existing chargers per thousand residents.

\textbf{District type.} Commercial, administrative, and educational districts generate
daytime and visitor demand that residential districts do not, so we map each type to a
propensity score from 0.3 (emerging residential) to 1.0 (commercial/administrative).

\subsection{Entropy-Weight Method}

Assigning equal weight to six indicators would ignore that districts differ sharply on
some dimensions and barely on others. An indicator on which all districts score similarly
provides little information for ranking. We use the entropy-weight method to derive
objective weights from the data \cite{Shannon1948}.

Let $X = [x_{ij}]_{m \times n}$ be the decision matrix with $m = 9$ districts and $n = 6$
indicators. We first normalize to remove scale differences. For benefit-type indicators
(direction $+$):

\begin{equation}
r_{ij} = \frac{x_{ij} - \min_i x_{ij}}{\max_i x_{ij} - \min_i x_{ij}}
\end{equation}

For cost-type indicators (direction $-$), we use the mirrored formula:

\begin{equation}
r_{ij} = \frac{\max_i x_{ij} - x_{ij}}{\max_i x_{ij} - \min_i x_{ij}}
\end{equation}

The normalized matrix $R = [r_{ij}]$ has entries in $[0,1]$ with larger values indicating
higher demand priority. The entropy of indicator $j$ is

\begin{equation}
p_{ij} = \frac{r_{ij}}{\sum_{i=1}^m r_{ij}}, \qquad
e_j = -\frac{1}{\ln m}\sum_{i=1}^m p_{ij}\ln p_{ij}
\end{equation}

with $p_{ij}\ln p_{ij}=0$ when $p_{ij}=0$. High entropy means districts score similarly on
that indicator, so it carries little discriminating information. The objective weight is

\begin{equation}
w_j = \frac{1 - e_j}{\sum_{k=1}^n (1 - e_k)}
\end{equation}

The weights sum to one and reflect each indicator's ability to separate districts.

\subsection{TOPSIS Ranking and Demand Priority Scores}

TOPSIS ranks districts by their geometric distance from an ideal and a negative-ideal
solution \cite{Hwang1981}. We form the weighted matrix $V = [v_{ij}]$ with
$v_{ij} = w_j r_{ij}$, and define the ideal $A^+$ and negative-ideal $A^-$ solutions:

\begin{equation}
A^+ = \{\max_i v_{i1}, \dots, \max_i v_{in}\}, \qquad
A^- = \{\min_i v_{i1}, \dots, \min_i v_{in}\}
\end{equation}

For each district $i$, the Euclidean distances to these references are

\begin{equation}
D_i^+ = \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^n (v_{ij} - A_j^+)^2}, \qquad
D_i^- = \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^n (v_{ij} - A_j^-)^2}
\end{equation}

The relative closeness to the ideal defines the demand priority score, rescaled to 0--100:

\begin{equation}
S_i = \frac{D_i^-}{D_i^+ + D_i^-} \times 100
\end{equation}

\subsection{Citywide EV Registration Forecast}

To plan expansion we must forecast how many EVs Springfield will have. The city holds only
six years of registrations, so a regression on a time index is unreliable: the series is
non-stationary, and its residuals are serially correlated. We therefore model the series as
a stochastic process with a classical ARIMA (autoregressive integrated moving-average)
model \cite{Box2015, Hyndman2018}, and we keep the logistic S-curve as a comparison
baseline.

We first choose the integration order $d$ with the Augmented Dickey--Fuller (ADF) test
\cite{Dickey1979}. On this short sample the test has little power, but it consistently
fails to reject a unit root in the level and in the first difference (both $p$-values near
$1.0$). We therefore treat the series as integrated of order two and set $d=2$. Let $C_t$
be citywide registrations in year $t$ and $\nabla^{2} C_t = C_t - 2C_{t-1} + C_{t-2}$ its
second difference. The general ARIMA$(p,d,q)$ model writes

\begin{equation}
\nabla^{d} C_t = \phi_1 \nabla^{d} C_{t-1} + \cdots + \phi_p \nabla^{d} C_{t-p}
              + \varepsilon_t + \theta_1 \varepsilon_{t-1} + \cdots + \theta_q \varepsilon_{t-q},
\end{equation}

where $\varepsilon_t$ is white noise. We select $p,q\in\{0,1\}$ by minimizing the Akaike
information criterion (AIC), which trades fit against model size. The chosen model is
ARIMA$(1,2,0)$ with AIC $=52.07$, coefficient $\hat\phi_1 = 0.935$, and residual standard
deviation $79$ vehicles. Figure \ref{fig:acfpacf} shows the ACF and PACF of the
second-differenced series used for this choice.

A pure ARIMA has no saturation ceiling and would grow without bound on a long horizon. We
therefore impose the hard business upper bound of $25{,}000$ vehicles from Assumption 2, so
the forecast cannot exceed the long-run ceiling the city's fleet size implies. Figure
\ref{fig:forecast} overlays the historical data, the ARIMA forecast with its 95\%
interval, and the logistic baseline, and Table \ref{tab:forecast} lists the point
forecasts.

\subsection{Site Attributes and Lexicographic Objectives}

A good location serves high demand, sits near other districts, and does not cost too much.
We therefore attach to each candidate site $s$ three quantities drawn directly from the
data in Tables \ref{tab:scores} and \ref{tab:degrees}.

\textbf{Demand coverage $D_s$.} The demand-priority score of the site's district, rescaled
to $[0,1]$ by dividing the 0--100 score by 100. A site in Downtown carries $D_s = 0.7545$;
a site in Riverside carries $D_s = 0.2595$.

\textbf{Degree centrality $C_s$.} The number of neighbors of the site's district in the
five-mile graph, normalized by the maximum degree of 4:

\begin{equation}
C_s = \frac{\deg(\text{district}(s))}{\max_j \deg(j)} = \frac{\deg_s}{4}.
\end{equation}

Downtown and University, at degree 4, receive $C_s = 1.0$; Industrial and Riverside, at
degree 1, receive $C_s = 0.25$. Degree centrality counts how many neighboring districts a
single station can serve through spillover, and it is the natural hub measure from the
graph constructed in Section 4.5.

\textbf{Installation cost $c_s$.} The one-time installation cost in units of \$10{,}000.
Cost is the scarce resource under the budget, so it enters the objective as a quantity to
minimize rather than as a scored criterion.

Two further attributes act as hard feasibility filters, not scored criteria. A site must be
developable and must be able to absorb the demand it is meant to serve:

\begin{equation}
L_s \ge 3 \qquad \text{(land availability at least 3 of 5)},
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
M_s \ge 4 \qquad \text{(capacity of at least 4 chargers)}.
\end{equation}

The land filter excludes S9 (Mall District), whose land score is 2, from the Phase-1
candidate set; every other site passes. The capacity filter is non-binding, because all ten
sites host at least four chargers. Table \ref{tab:sites} lists the site-level data and

\subsection{Lexicographic Integer Programming Formulation}

The three objectives, demand, centrality, and cost, are not combined by an arbitrary
weighted sum. Instead we solve them in a strict priority order: demand coverage is the
primary goal, centrality breaks ties among equally good demand solutions, and cost breaks
ties among solutions equal on both. Let $x_s \in \{0,1\}$ indicate whether site $s$ is
selected. We write the optimization as a lexicographic maximum of three ordered terms:

\begin{equation}
\operatorname*{lex\,max}_{x} \;\Big(\;\sum_{s=1}^{10} D_s x_s,\quad \sum_{s=1}^{10} C_s x_s,\quad -\sum_{s=1}^{10} c_s x_s\;\Big)
\end{equation}

subject to

\begin{equation}
\sum_{s=1}^{10} c_s x_s \le 200 \qquad \text{(budget: \$2,000,000)}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
4 \le \sum_{s=1}^{10} x_s \le 6 \qquad \text{(station count)}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
L_s \ge 3 x_s, \qquad M_s \ge 4 x_s \qquad \text{(land and capacity filters)}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\sum_{s\,:\,\operatorname{dist}(s,j)\,\le\,5} x_s \;\ge\; 1, \qquad j=1,\dots,9 \qquad \text{(coverage)}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
x_s \in \{0,1\}, \qquad s=1,\dots,10
\end{equation}

Lexicographic maximization works as follows. Among all feasible selections, keep only
those maximizing $\sum D_s x_s$ (Level 1); among those survivors, those maximizing
$\sum C_s x_s$ (Level 2); among those, those minimizing $\sum c_s x_s$ (Level 3). This
yields a single answer with no subjective trade-off parameter.

The coverage constraint uses the distance matrix directly: every district must lie within
five miles of at least one selected station. This is weaker than a station in every
district, which is impossible with at most six sites, yet it guarantees no district lacks
a nearby charger. Because Riverside's only neighbor is Westside, the coverage constraint
forces Westside into the Phase-1 set whenever Riverside is not selected.

With ten candidate sites the feasible set has at most
$\binom{10}{4}+\binom{10}{5}+\binom{10}{6}=672$ combinations, so we enumerate every
feasible combination and compare their lexicographic tuples, guaranteeing global
optimality.

\section{Task 3: Future Expansion Planning}

\subsection{Break-Even Analysis for Phase Timing}

The Phase-1 selection answers where to build first. To decide \emph{when} to build the next
stations we need a data-driven trigger rather than an arbitrary EV threshold. We derive the
trigger from a break-even calculation for each site.

A station breaks even when the revenue it collects from the EVs it serves covers its
annualized cost. Let $c_s$ be the installation cost and $m_s$ the annual maintenance cost
of site $s$. Amortizing installation over a ten-year horizon at $\alpha=0.1$, and assuming
each EV contributes $R=\$50$ per year in charging revenue, the break-even number of EVs is

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{BE}_s = \frac{\alpha\, c_s + m_s}{R} = \frac{0.1\, c_s + m_s}{50}.
\end{equation}

Table \ref{tab:breakeven} reports $\mathrm{BE}_s$ for every site. Low-cost, low-maintenance
sites break even at a few hundred EVs; the most expensive site, S9, needs 1,600.

\begin{equation}

\subsection{Multi-Period Integer Program}

We now replace the ad hoc phase assignment with a single optimization over all three
phases. Let $x_{s,t}\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether site $s$ is built in phase
$t\in\{1,2,3\}$. Stations built earlier serve demand sooner, so we discount phase-$t$
demand by $\delta^{t}$ with $\delta=0.95$:

\begin{equation}
\max \sum_{t=1}^{3}\sum_{s=1}^{10} \delta^{t}\, D_s\, x_{s,t}
\end{equation}

subject to

\begin{equation}
\sum_{s=1}^{10} c_s\, x_{s,t} \le B_t, \qquad (B_1, B_2, B_3) = (200, 90, 90) \times \$10{,}000
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
4 \le \sum_{s=1}^{10} x_{s,1} \le 6, \qquad 2 \le \sum_{s=1}^{10} x_{s,t} \le 4 \quad (t = 2,3)
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\sum_{t=1}^{3} x_{s,t} \le 1 \;\; \forall s, \qquad \sum_{t=1}^{3}\sum_{s=1}^{10} x_{s,t} \le 10
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\sum_{t=1}^{3}\sum_{s\,:\,\operatorname{dist}(s,j)\,\le\,5} x_{s,t} \;\ge\; 1 \;\; \forall j
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
x_{s,t} \in \{0,1\}.
\end{equation}

The first constraint caps spending in each phase. The second bounds the number of stations
per phase, with Phase 1 inheriting the four-to-six range from Task 2. The third ensures
each site is built at most once and that at most ten sites are built in total. The fourth
is the coverage constraint: by the end of Phase 3 every district lies within five miles of
a built station. Enumerating the $4^{10}$ phase assignments yields the optimum in Table
\ref{tab:multiperiod}.

relative differences that are stable under uniform scaling. The plan is robust in structure
and only mildly sensitive in timing.

\section{Model Evaluation}

\subsection{Strengths}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Objective indicator weighting.} The entropy-weight method derives
    indicator importance from the data rather than expert judgment, making the demand
    ranking reproducible and free of subjective bias.
    \item \textbf{Provably optimal location selection.} Because the candidate set is small,
    both the Phase-1 and multi-period programs are solved by complete enumeration. The
    reported selections are globally optimal, not heuristic guesses.
    \item \textbf{No arbitrary trade-off weights.} The lexicographic ordering replaces the
    weighted utility sum, so the site selection depends on no subjective relative weight
    between demand, centrality, and cost. Each objective enters only in a strict, defensible
    priority order.
    \item \textbf{Data-driven phase timing.} The break-even rule ties each build phase to a
    computed utilization threshold rather than an arbitrary EV number, and the multi-period
    program optimizes all three phases jointly.
    \item \textbf{Computational efficiency.} The entire pipeline executes in a fraction of
    a second, allowing interactive what-if analysis by city planners.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations and Improvement Schemes}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Short sample and imposed ceiling.} The ARIMA forecast is fit on only six
    annual observations, so the ADF test has little power and the chosen order is sensitive
    to the small sample. The 25,000 ceiling is a business judgment imposed after fitting,
    not a fitted parameter. Testing a range of ceilings (15,000--35,000) and orders would
    quantify how these choices shift the forecast; over 2025--2027 the forecast stays below
    both the ceiling and the logistic alternative, so the near-term roadmap timing is
    stable.
    \item \textbf{Qualitative district-type mapping.} The district-type propensity is a
    judgment-based 0--1 scale, though it is justified by the infrastructure literature cited
    in Section 3. It could be replaced with measured proxies such as employment density or
    point-of-interest counts.
    \item \textbf{Enumeration does not scale.} The multi-period program enumerates all
    $4^{10}$ phase assignments. This is exact for ten sites but would not scale to a city
    with many more candidates or phases; a branch-and-bound or column-generation method
    would be needed for larger instances.
    \item \textbf{No queuing or utilization modeling.} We do not model waiting times,
    charger utilization rates, or station saturation. A queuing-based extension would
    refine capacity decisions where demand is densest.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusions}

We developed a quantitative framework for deploying Springfield's public EV charging
network. Our main contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A demand assessment model} that scores nine districts on six indicators
    using entropy weights and TOPSIS. Downtown ranks first (75.45) and Riverside last
    (25.95), with a clear, explainable ranking in between.
    \item \textbf{A lexicographic location model} that selects six Phase-1 stations
    (S1--S6) across five districts at \$1.98 million, provably maximizing demand coverage
    with centrality and cost as tie-breakers.
    \item \textbf{A connectivity and coverage analysis} showing the district network is
    fully connected, with Downtown and University as the highest-degree hubs, and
    demonstrating that forcing a University station would reduce demand coverage by
    8.43\%.
    \item \textbf{An ARIMA EV forecast} projecting 3,242 vehicles in 2025, 6,083 in 2027,
    and 11,915 in 2030, driving a demand-ordered roadmap.
    \item \textbf{A phased deployment plan} solved by a discounted multi-period integer
    program, growing from 6 to 8 to 10 stations, with each phase timed by a break-even
    threshold, achieving full nine-district coverage by 2030.
    \item \textbf{A data-exploration and break-even methodology} that screens the raw data
    for missing values, outliers, and collinearity, and derives phase triggers from station
    economics rather than arbitrary thresholds.
    \item \textbf{A robustness analysis} showing the plan's structure is stable under
    $\pm$20\% growth error and budget cuts, with only the marginal station at risk when
    costs rise.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{What we found.} Demand is concentrated. Downtown needs charging most: it has the

\textbf{What we recommend.} Build in three steps. First, in 2025--2026, build six

\section*{Appendix: Python Implementation}

\textbf{Dependencies:} Python 3.13+, NumPy 2.5+, SciPy 1.18+, Matplotlib 3.11+.
Complete source code: \texttt{ev\_charging\_model.py} (supplementary material).

\vspace{4pt}

\noindent\textbf{Core Module Architecture.} The codebase implements the deployment model
in eight modules, each reproducing a numbered result in this paper.

\texttt{data\_exploration()} checks for missing values, computes indicator Z-scores to flag
outliers, and builds the correlation matrix. It produces Figure \ref{fig:correlation}.

\texttt{demand\_model()} normalizes the six-indicator decision matrix with
direction-dependent min-max formulas, computes indicator entropies, and returns the
entropy weights and TOPSIS demand scores. This function produces Tables
\ref{tab:weights} and \ref{tab:scores}.

\texttt{ev\_forecast\_arima()} runs the ADF stationarity test, selects the ARIMA order by
minimum AIC, and forecasts registrations through 2030 with a 25,000 cap. The companion
\texttt{ev\_forecast()} keeps the logistic S-curve as a comparison baseline, and
\texttt{compare\_forecasts()} prints the side-by-side table. Together they produce Table
\ref{tab:forecast} and Figures \ref{fig:forecast} and \ref{fig:acfpacf}.

\texttt{location\_model()} attaches demand coverage, degree centrality, and installation
cost to each site, then solves the lexicographic zero-one integer program by exhaustive
enumeration over all combinations of four to six sites under the budget, land, capacity,
and coverage constraints. It produces Tables \ref{tab:sites} and \ref{tab:phase1}.

\texttt{graph\_model()} constructs the undirected distance-threshold graph (edges at
$\leq 5$ miles), computes node degrees via the adjacency matrix, and finds connected
components by breadth-first search. It produces Table \ref{tab:degrees}.

\texttt{break\_even()} computes each site's break-even EV count and the citywide Phase-2
and Phase-3 thresholds. It produces Table \ref{tab:breakeven}.

\texttt{multi\_period()} solves the discounted three-phase integer program over all
$4^{10}$ phase assignments. It produces Table \ref{tab:multiperiod}.

\texttt{sensitivity()} re-solves the lexicographic program under scaled growth, scaled
costs, reduced budgets, and forced-station constraints, producing Figure
\ref{fig:sensitivity} and Tables \ref{tab:costsens}, \ref{tab:budget}, and
\ref{tab:whatif}.

All experiments are deterministic (no random seeds, no unordered-set iteration). Execute
\texttt{python3 ev\_charging\_model.py} to reproduce every number and figure in this
paper.
